L31 — Sustainable Transportation Planning & Approaches-II: Contemporary Planning Process

Planning background

  • s3 Common flaws in planning based only on forecasting & facility expansion (graph labels): A early-stage forecasting (under-estimation); B late-stage forecasting (over-estimation); C "Return to normal" bias. (Graph also shows "forecasting reference year", "forecasting gap", "conventional growth cycle".)
  • s4 Main challenges (UNESCAP 2015): non-inclusive development; difficult to meet growing demand (economic & population growth, rising income – faster than growth of economies); air pollution & GHG emissions growing faster than GDP; adverse health & social impacts; road safety.
  • s5 U.K. Transport Planning Society view – planning should: prepare, assess, implement policies, plans, projects; improve & manage transport systems efficiently; plan at local, regional, national level; understand interrelationship between transport infrastructure and future shape of cities/towns; change people's perspectives/attitude and encourage alternative modes rather than private cars.
  • s6 Sustainable transportation: capacity to support the mobility needs of a society in a manner that is the least damageable to the environment and does not impair the mobility needs of future generations.
  • s7 Sustainable Development → Sustainable Transportation (linked with sustainable transport Modes, Infrastructures, Operations). Dimensions: Environment (climate change, air quality, noise, land use, waste); Economy (materials & energy, growth, employment, pricing, competitiveness); Society (safety, health, disturbance, access, equity).

The contemporary transport planning process

  • s8 Commonly scale-specific and multi-dimensional. Traffic problems increased significantly since the 1970s despite much urban planning. Early planning was based on induced demand (to enhance capacities) rather than better management/maintenance of existing systems. Preference: Accessibility rather than Mobility.
  • s9–10 Multi-stage process (5 stages): 1) Goals & Objectives – accessibility with improving safety & health, reducing vehicular emissions, enhancing economic opportunities, improving livability, promoting mobility by improving equity. 2) Set of possible options – what objectives are desirable & what they imply; several alternate scenarios considered. 3) Identification of actors, institutions & stakeholders – those affected, possible resolutions, public participation. 4) Predicting outcomes, identifying benefits, assessing costs – critical step; e.g. visual impacts, environmental dislocations, employment impacts. 5) Choosing a course of action – evaluating scenarios (costs & benefits); political decisions.
  • s11 Urban transport development paths: development takes place within a path dependency → different levels of mobility & ownership (cars, buses, rail transit); once a path is elected, future development is locked in.
  • s12 Benefits of reducing car dependency: healthier & active lifestyle; reduces demand for car parking; improves air & noise quality; improves safety on roads; reduction in traffic levels; provides improved alternative choices for travel; improves accessibility to destinations by all modes.
  • s13–14 Planning: dichotomy → continuum. Megalopolises (mega-urban regions) = massive urban conurbations often encompassing several EMRs, structured along a corridor. Ex.: BostWash (Boston–Washington), Tokaido (Tokyo–Osaka), Pearl River Delta (Hong Kong–Guangzhou), Shanghai–Nanjing. Spatial imprint of transport varies with socio-economic & geographical characteristics; spatial interaction depends on circulation pattern of passengers & freight. Ex.: North American cities – urban form shaped by the automobile, dominance of highways.
  • s15 City connectivity from the CBD (CBD = Central Business District); map legend groups: Production & Distribution (port, heavy industry, railyards, manufacturing, distribution clusters); Mobility & Accessibility (transit stations, shopping, airport districts); Transactions (financial/management districts).
  • s16 Cities may be Polycentric (more common) or Monocentric. Traffic flows may be Organized or disorganized (more common).
  • s17 Street networks: Grid pattern was relatively not common in 19th & early 20th century. Grid: reduction in level of connectivity & level of landuse [as stated]. Cul-de-sac planning reduces/eliminates through movements; cul-de-sacs = dead-end streets reducing extra traffic movement mainly in residential areas.
  • s18 One-hour commuting by different modes: a theoretical urban form = summation of successive transport influences; assumes uniform friction of distance and capacity of transport infrastructure.

Achieving sustainable urban transport planning s21–23

  • Framework diagram (Gudmundsson 2014): Governance (political, institutional, regulatory) → System (infrastructure, energy, vehicles) and Demand (land use, economy, life style) → Activity (distance, mode, speed) → Impacts (economic, social, environmental).
  • Governance of transport infrastructure is critical because of its strategic, economic & social importance. Interrelationship between transport modes and infrastructures should be considered in planning.

Transport Demand Management (TDM) s24–25

  • Manage both demand and the transport system, not only demand as in earlier planning. High population densities favour walking, cycling, public transport. Densification & integration: planning concentrated along well-served transport corridors (TOD – transit-oriented development) and increasing densities in areas under rehabilitation.
  • TDM is done through (1) traffic calming measures and (2) traffic management strategies (continued in L32).

Traffic calming measures s26–61

  • Main idea: reduce speed of flowing traffic or reduce cut-through traffic by obstructing movements in one or more directions. Four general types: Vertical deflections, Horizontal shifts, Narrowing roadways (these three intended to reduce speed) and Closures (intended to reduce cut-through traffic).

Curb extension / bulb-out s31–33Horizontal extension of sidewalk into street → narrower roadway; easier/safer pedestrian crossing (shortens curb-to-curb distance); at mid-block = choker. Gives space for benches & street lighting.
Diagonal diverter s34–36Barriers placed diagonally across four-legged intersections, blocking through movements; also full diverters / diagonal road closures; can be partial closure allowing only two-wheelers and obstructing four-wheelers.
Lateral shift s37–38Realignment of straight street so travel lanes shift in ≥1 direction. Suitable: local, collector, arterial roads; one-lane one-way & two-lane two-way; with/without bicycle facilities; max appropriate speed limit typically 35 mph; along bus transit routes.

Half closure: barrier blocks travel in one direction (creates one-way street) for a short distance on two-way street; also partial closures / one-way closures.
Median barrier / forced turn island s44–46Raised islands along street centreline continuing through an intersection; blocks through movement from cross street; also called median diverter, intersection barrier, intersection diverter, island diverter. (Examples: with pedestrian refuge & bicycle cut-through.)
Median island s47–48Raised island along centreline that narrows travel lanes at that location; same alternate names; separates opposing traffic and slows by briefly narrowing roadway; may have crosswalk.
Mini roundabout s49–50Raised islands in unsignalized intersections around which traffic circulates; drivers slow to comfortably maneuver.
Roundabout s51–52Raised islands large in diameter, unsignalized intersections; can serve high volumes of large trucks & buses depending on design; different from traffic circles or mini-roundabouts; possible substitute for traffic signal control; encourages steady flow; may have statues, fountains, community gardens.
On-street parking s53–55Allocation of paved space to parking; narrows travel lanes, increases side friction; one or both sides; parallel or angled, but parallel generally preferred for maximized speed reduction.
Raised pedestrian crossing / intersection s56–57Flat raised areas covering entire intersections, ramps on all approaches, often brick/textured; raised to sidewalk height → slows vehicles, easier crossing; also called raised junctions, intersection humps, plateaus; same-level crossing for all pedestrians & specially abled.
Road diet s58–61Revision of lane use/widths → one travel lane per direction with minimum practical width, goal: reduce cross-section. Ex.: four-lane two-way road → three-lane road (two through lanes + centre two-way left-turn lane, TWLTL). May narrow lanes; alternate uses: bicycle facilities, left-turn lanes, on-street parking, raised medians, pedestrian refuge islands, sidewalks. Seattle Rainier Avenue: 4-mile dangerous lane, drivers at 40 mph+; after modification speeds decreased by approx. 95% [as stated].

Conclusion s62

  • Contemporary planning ensures sustainable development by emphasising accessibility rather than mobility; focus on management of existing traffic & infrastructure rather than mere capacity enhancement; traffic calming ensures safe planning by controlling traffic speed and volume; traffic management strategies should be combined with calming and policy measures.
Important Distinctions to Remember (L31)
  • Hump vs bump: hump = gradual, residential/local, ~30 km/h; bump = abrupt, more aggressive, parking lots/driveways.
  • Speed table = flat top + ramps; raised crosswalk (at pedestrian crossing) vs offset speed table (one direction only).
  • Curb extension at mid-block = choker. Full closure (cul-de-sac/mini-park) vs half closure (one-way).
  • Lateral shift (single realignment, ≤35 mph) vs chicane (series of alternating curves).
  • Mini roundabout vs roundabout (large diameter; substitute for signals). Calming types: 3 reduce speed, closures reduce cut-through traffic.

L32 — Sustainable Transportation Planning & Approaches-III: Management Strategies

Transportation System Management (TSM) s3

  • Solution to peak-hour congestion, delay, accidents in cities. Widening roads, elevated flyovers, bypasses, urban expressways may prove costly.
  • TSM: a set of measures adopted to ensure the most productive and cost-effective use of existing transportation services and modes.
  • Strategies covered (s2): prohibited turning; one-way streets; car pooling; staggering office hours; chartered buses; peripheral parking; internal CBD shuttle; transport rationing; congestion pricing; parking restrictions; day-time truck restriction; real-time travel information; contraflow lanes; exclusive bus lanes.

Benefits: increased automobile occupancy; decrease in person trips; faster travel & less delay; more public transport use; less air pollution; low ambient noise. Limitations: followed in Paris, Rome, Bogota, Mexico City, Beijing; mostly failed after initial temporary success; people buy a cheap secondary car; low public transport capacity/poor infrastructure causes distress. Only-pedestrian on weekends: for very high footfall places; Connaught Place (more than a million footfall daily); NDMC planned to restrict vehicles Saturdays & Sundays in inner circle → trader protest → rolled back. No-Car Sundays, Jakarta (capital of Indonesia): two Sundays a month; "Car-Free Sunday"; concept gaining traction incl. India & Singapore.

Benefits: increase system efficiency & capacity; enhance mobility; improve safety; reduce energy consumption & environmental costs; increase economic productivity.
Contraflow lanes s39–43Lane made exclusively for peak-hour traffic in one direction; better connectivity, less congestion/travel time. Ex.: single lane of two-way road towards business park is contraflow in morning peak, opposite direction in evening peak. Usually for public transport, emergency vehicles, bicyclists. Examples: contraflow bus lane; contraflow bicycle lane on one-way street; Dublin (allowing entry in opposite direction only for cyclists).
Exclusive bus lanes s44–46Reserve a lane exclusively for buses; possible only where adequate carriageway width (minimum 3 lanes); adjacent to curb to board & embark; exclusive bus/cycle lanes may also be contraflow lanes. Example: Ahmedabad.

Taming the complexity / Conclusions s47–48

  • Sustainable transportation possible only with integrated effort of individuals, families, organizations, governments at local, regional, state, national levels.
  • TSM ensures productive & cost-effective solution to peak congestion, delay, accidents; right strategy or combination gives good results; reduces private vehicles, congestion, delays, fuel cost, emissions.
Important Distinctions to Remember (L32)
  • T-turn (alternative intersection) vs G-turn (left before junction) vs Q-turn (beyond junction).
  • Contraflow lane (peak-hour, opposite direction) vs exclusive bus lane (needs minimum 3 lanes, next to curb); both may be contraflow.
  • Car pooling types: casual, real-time (app), van pooling (7–15). Park & ride = outskirts parking + public transport; CBD shuttle = uninterrupted shuttle within the city.
  • Congestion pricing (fine for peak-hour use; London 2003 cordon; Singapore) vs parking restrictions (indirect reduction) vs rationing (odd-even; fails with second cheap car).

L33 — Life Cycle Assessment (LCA): An Introduction

Definitions & objectives

  • s3 LCA: systematic analysis of environmental impact over the entire life cycle of a product, material, process or other measurable activity. LCA models the environmental implications of the many interacting systems that make up industrial production.
  • s4 Life cycle of a product/service: overall span from extraction of raw material → application by user → waste and recycle.

System boundaries s9–19

  • Defined by cut-off criteria (define parts/materials included in and excluded from the product system). Depends on study objective; often a combination of cut-off criteria; vary with life-cycle stages considered. Four main options: Cradle to Grave, Cradle to Gate, Gate to Grave, Gate to Gate.
  • Linear production model: raw-material extraction → waste generation in linear fashion. Circular production model: includes recovery of material after usage; considered more environment-friendly than linear.

Stages of LCA s20–21

  1. Defining goal & scope
  2. LC inventory analysis (LCI)
  3. Impact assessment
  4. Interpretation

LCA process diagram (s20, US EPA 1993): inputs = raw materials, energy → within system boundary: raw materials acquisition → manufacturing → use/reuse/maintenance → recycle/waste management → outputs = atmospheric emissions, waterborne wastes, solid wastes, coproducts, other releases.

1. Goal & scope s22–28

  • Scope items (8): functional unit; reference flow; description of the system; system boundaries; allocation procedures; impact categories & impact assessment method; data requirements; data quality requirements.
  • Functional unit: quantified definition of the function of a product. Reference flow: measure of product components & materials needed to fulfil the function defined by the functional unit; all LCA data must be calculated/scaled per this reference flow.
  • Data requirement: energy inputs; raw material inputs; ancillary inputs; products & co-products; wastes; emissions to air, water and soil; other environmental aspects.
  • Factors affecting data quality (1/2): data acquisition (measured/calculated/estimated? % primary vs secondary data from literature & databases); time-reference (when obtained, major changes since); geographical reference (country/region). (2/2): technology (state-of-the-art vs older); precision; completeness (missing data, how gaps filled); representativeness, consistency, reproducibility.
  • Allocation = partitioning/relating inputs & outputs of a process to relevant products and byproducts. By mass (proportional to mass); by heating value (often used for production processes of fuels); by market value.

2. Life cycle inventory (LCI) s29–31

  • Inputs & outputs of an industrial system are measured and recorded according to the functional unit; by end of phase an inventory list detailing all input/output data is created. Typically illustrated with a flow model (tables alone cause confusion).
  • Example – cement (cradle-to-gate total per metric tonne of production): global warming potential (100 yr) 1040 kg CO₂-eq; acidification 2.45 kg SO₂-eq; eutrophication 1.22 kg N-eq; tropospheric ozone formation 48.8 kg O₃-eq; ozone depletion 2.61E-05 kg CFC11-eq; non-renewable primary energy fossil 5250 MJ, nuclear 345 MJ; renewable (solar, wind, hydro, geothermal) 127 MJ, biomass 165 MJ.

3. Impact assessment (LCIA) s32–35

  • Definition: evaluating the collected inventory data to make it meaningful in context of potential damage to environment or human health; impacts assessed by impact category.
  • Impact categories: Global warming – rise in global mean temperature due to GHG emissions; Acidification – excess CO₂ → ocean pH decreasing, affecting marine life; Eutrophication – excess algae in water body impacting ecology; Smog creation – impacts human health; Ozone depletion – ozone layer depletes, UV filtration decreases → skin diseases.
  • US EPA LCIA steps (7): selection & definition of impact categories → classification → characterization → normalization → grouping → weighting → evaluating & reporting LCIA results.
  • Example table (s35): GWP – kg CO₂ eq; acidification – kg SO₂ eq; eutrophication – kg N eq; smog creation – kg O₃ eq; ozone depletion – kg CFC-11 eq. Outputs are converted into a few categories and impacts assessed.

4. Interpretation s36

  • (i) Identify significant issues based on LCI and LCIA; (ii) evaluate the study – completeness, sensitivity, consistency, account for uncertainty; (iii) conclusions, limitations, recommendations.

s37 Overview figure: impact assessment (global warming, ozone depletion, summer smog, eutrophication, human toxicity, eco-toxicity, land use, resource consumption) ← life cycle inventory ← steps: raw material extraction, production of intermediates, production of main products, utilization, recycling/recovery/deposition.

Conclusions s38

  • LCA = method to assess impact of product/service on environment; serves objectives from alternative selection to marketing; multi-step: goal & scope, inventory, assessment, interpretation.
Important Distinctions to Remember (L33)
  • Cradle-to-gate (production only up to factory gate) vs gate-to-grave (use + end-of-life) vs gate-to-gate (production phase only) vs cradle-to-grave (linear, whole) vs cradle-to-cradle (circular, closed-loop, recycling instead of waste).
  • Functional unit (quantified function) vs reference flow (components/materials needed to fulfil it).
  • Allocation: mass vs heating value (fuels) vs market value.
  • Well-to-wheel = transport fuels & vehicles; EIOLCA = sector averages, not precise at product level.

Case study: LCA of last-mile distribution logistics, Milan & Turin (Italy) s13–32

  • LTZ: regulatory intervention to improve air quality, lower noise, make area pedestrian-friendly; generally in high-footfall urban centres; vehicle traffic allowed only in limited off-peak hours or few vehicle categories exempted. LTZs = important area of analysis of delivery activities by logistics operators.
  • Details: assesses environmental footprint of last-mile logistics by LCA; delivery activities in LTZ by a large private logistics operator; scenario analysis: current practice vs replacing diesel fleet with electric vehicles (EV).

  • Goal – scenarios: Baseline = typical day of a single large private operator, exclusively diesel vehicles; ZEV (Zero Emission Vehicle) scenario = diesel vehicles replaced by EVs. (EV = electric vehicles; LTZ = Limited Traffic Zone.)

L36 — Material Flow Analysis (MFA)

Concept, definition, history s3–8

  • MFA is based on the law of mass conservation: matter can change form through physical & chemical changes but is conserved; mass going into a system = mass coming out + mass absorbed (stored) in the system. Law dates from Antoine Lavoisier's 1789 discovery (mass neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions).
  • MFA definition (Brunner & Rechberger, 2004): systematic assessment of the flows and stocks of materials within a system defined in space and time.
  • Equation: Input = Output + Stock. Example: 100 L fuel × 2 g lead/L = Input 200 g; Output 150 g lead in air; Stock = 200 − 150 = 50 g (remains within system).
  • History: conservation of matter (input = output) first postulated by Greek philosophers more than 2000 years ago; mass-balance principle applied in medicine, chemistry, economics, engineering, life sciences. Two original application areas: metabolism of cities; analysis of pollutant pathways in regions such as watersheds or urban areas.
  • Objectives (1/2, 2/2): delineate system of material flows & stocks; reduce system complexity while maintaining basis for decision-making; assess relevant flows & stocks quantitatively (check mass balance, sensitivities, uncertainties); present results in reproducible, understandable, transparent fashion; use results as basis for managing resources, environment, wastes; monitor accumulation/depletion of stocks, future environmental loadings; design environmentally-beneficial goods, processes, systems. Examples: hazardous substance causing health issues – create flow & stock of incoming/outgoing/stocking; PM10 vehicular emission – study environmental loading in a city region.

L38 — Concept of Circular Economy

Economy & linear model s3–6

  • Economy: concerned with production, distribution, consumption of goods & services; studies how individuals, businesses, governments, nations allocate resources; considered at national level but also local, regional, global; all economies interlinked ("global village"); general parameter = GDP; components: money supply, commercial activities/services, industry.
  • Linear model: take–make–consume–throw away; based on "higher the consumption – bigger the economy".
  • Drawbacks of linear economy: 2–5% of global GDP lost due to congestion; roads capture more urban land; worsening air quality; one third of food produced globally (worth USD 1 trillion) wasted each year while more than 10% of world population goes hungry. (Infographic s5: 10–15% of building material wasted during construction; 60% of European offices not used even in working hours; 20–40% of energy in existing buildings can be profitably conserved; 54% of demolition materials landfilled while some countries only landfill 6%.)
  • s7 Conceptual solution: minimize consumption & wastage; optimum utilization of resources; regenerate & reuse products/services; return resources to environment → model = Circular Economy (CE).

Circular economy: definition, principles, features s8–12

  • Definition: a systemic approach to economic development designed to benefit businesses, society and environment; a production & consumption model of reusing, repairing, refurbishing, recycling existing materials/products to keep materials within the economy wherever possible.
  • Three principles of CE: Design out waste and pollution; Keep products and materials in use; Regenerate natural systems.
  • Features: economy that is restorative and regenerative by design; economic activity builds & rebuilds overall system health; waste becomes a resource (minimizing waste); works at all scales (big/small business, organizations, individuals, global/local).
  • Linear vs circular: Take–make–dispose vs Make–use–return or make–consume–enrich. [Diagram: linear – technical & biological materials mixed up, energy from finite sources; circular – biological & technical materials, energy from renewable sources.] Conceptual diagram stages: raw materials, design, production & remanufacturing, distribution, consumption/use/reuse/repair, collection, recycling, residual waste.

Benefits s31–33

  • Economic: job creation (labor-intensive recycling; higher-skilled manufacturing jobs; new service-based economy activities); innovation (recycling, remanufacturing, resource-savvy production); material cost saving; economic growth (increased revenues & more productive utilization of inputs).
  • Environmental: contributes to global climate targets by designing out waste & pollution, keeping products/materials in use, regenerating rather than degrading natural systems.
  • For industries: creation of new profit opportunities; reduced costs due to lower virgin-material requirements; stronger customer relationships via responsible/environment-friendly practices.

Linear → circular; Indian practices s34–38

  • System thinking: business models, design, legislation, planning, practices, manufacturing all have undesirable qualities from circular view; cannot change just one element → need holistic approach rather than micro interventions.
  • Ancient practices in India: domestic animals part of family; organic fertilizers (dung & organic waste) before chemical fertilizers; sacredness of trees; rivers, forests, earth treated like living being (now "system metabolism"); affection for ancestral property protects wells, resources, land.
  • Vedic Darshan: "Mata Bhumi putroham prithivyah" = Earth is my mother, I am her son; Vedas hymns pray to Indra not to separate trees from forests and sons from fathers (Rig-veda 8/1/13); Vṛkṣāyurveda: planting a tree equally beneficial as having ten offspring.
  • Buddhism: environment not just to serve humans; humans only a part, no supremacy; eco-centric philosophy; per Kutadanta, ruler's responsibility to protect flora & fauna.
  • Tagore: "highest education... makes our life in harmony with all existence". Mahatma Gandhi: Gram Swarajya – self-reliance & environment-friendly development of rural economy.

Conclusions s39

  • Linear model → unsustainable; CE can replace it; CE based on older time-tested concepts; ensures environmental, economic, industrial benefits; holistic approach needed.
Important Distinctions to Remember (L38)
  • Linear (take-make-consume-throw away) vs circular (make-use-return / make-consume-enrich).
  • Biological cycle (green, left, nutrients/biodegradation) vs technological cycle (blue, right, metals/plastics/synthetic chemicals recycle continuously).
  • Refurbish (no disassembly, cosmetic) vs remanufacture (full disassembly, parts replaced) vs recycle (back to basic material, low value).
  • Cradle to cradle (waste = nutrients, 7 bases) vs biomimicry (model/measure/mentor) vs industrial ecology (closed-loop, waste = input) vs blue economy (21 principles, gravity) vs regenerative design (agriculture).

L40 — Modelling of Transport Emissions-I

Vehicular emissions & inventory s3–8

  • Three types of vehicular emissions: Exhaust, Evaporative, Non-exhaust (tyre and brake).
  • Exhaust: CO, HC, NOx, CO₂, PM, benzene, 1-3 butadiene, formaldehyde, PAH (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons), acetaldehyde, total aldehyde, GHG emissions. Evaporative: VOCs/hydrocarbons. Non-exhaust: tyre & brake emissions – PM2.5 and PM10, heavy metals.
  • Emission inventory = important tool to assess vehicular emissions based on emission factors, average speed, fuel consumption and amount of traffic on a defined road type; developed manually or by computer-based models. Models developed on: vehicle type; fuel category & engine technology; vehicle kilometers travelled (VKT); emission standards; various correction factors.
  • Decision Support System (DSS) framework: model-based emission inventory development → data analysis/action to implement policy → traffic development & policy implementation → evaluation of efficiency of policies. Emission model = summation of product of emissions from all vehicular sources, vehicular activities and climatic & topographic variables.
  • Model inputs: vehicle population, type, age, model, technology, fuel type, VKT, temperature, humidity, altitude. Outputs: emission inventory by pollutants; vehicle-type contribution; efficiency of current policy; input data for dispersion models; vehicular emission trends; fuel consumption & contribution.

Indian context s39–42

  • Pune example: dominant vehicles two-wheelers followed by three-wheelers; buses and light-duty four-wheelers major NOx source.
  • Model complexity & errors: reliability depends on accuracy and robustness; accuracy improved by more complex formulations, but robustness tends to be negated by increasing input errors.
  • Limitations for India: MOBILE, MOVES, COPERT etc. developed in developed countries (US, Japan, Australia) with extensive datasets; differences in technology, infrastructure, driving cycle → may give erroneous results; emission factors/basic emission rates are US & Europe based; IVE designed for developing countries like India but complexity similar to US/European models (extensive data, hard to compile); most models cannot give output for more than a year; need experimental data not available for India; most are region specific.

VAPI model (Vehicular Air Pollution Inventory, IIT Roorkee) s43–54

  • Designed specifically for developing countries like India; incorporates climatic correction factors; fair agreement with Indian air-quality observations; simple to use, public domain.
  • Assumptions: vehicles per 1000 persons grows in a Sigmoidal (S-shaped) relationship; saturation = one car plus two wheelers per family; seating capacities of auto-rickshaws & taxis 3 and 5 → saturation per 1000 persons 330 and 200. Vehicle population projection based on GDP and per capita income using the Gompertz model; on-road population = projected past-year population subtracted from cumulative registered population; base emission factors depend on vehicle age and model year.
  • Pollutants: running exhaust – CO, HC, NOx, CO₂, PM, benzene, 1-3 butadiene, formaldehyde, total PAH, acetaldehyde, total aldehyde; running loss evaporative emissions (VOC); non-exhaust (brakes, tyres) – PM10 & PM2.5.
  • Running exhaust: EF_a,p,i = EF_p,i × C_T × C_H × C_A (temperature, humidity, altitude correction factors); E_t,p,i = P_i × EF_a,p,i × V_i × D_t,i (P = on-road population; V = per day distance km/day; D = annual travelling days). Evaporative: EF_a = EF × C_T × C_A (temperature & altitude only); running losses from petrol vehicles are largest source of VOC evaporative emissions (others: diurnal, hot soak, resting losses); running losses = vapor generated in gasoline tanks during vehicle operation. Non-exhaust PM: generated mechanically by abrasion of tyre, brake, road pavement and resuspension of road dust.

Conclusion s55

  • Models mainly developed by US, Europe, Australia; complex, predict emissions of all vehicle categories; but mostly country specific and based on extensive native datasets; VAPI is a simple model designed for developing countries like India.
Important Distinctions to Remember (L40)
  • Static = top-down = macro (aggregate emission factor, average speed) vs dynamic = bottom-up = micro (traffic situation, instantaneous).
  • MOBILE (superseded; US except California) → MOVES (current; US except California) vs EMFAC (California) vs COPERT (EEA, Europe) vs QGEPA (Queensland, Australia) vs IVE (developing countries; UC Riverside etc.; USEPA-funded) vs VAPI (IIT Roorkee, India).
  • MOBILE ex = SCF (BER + EO) vs EMFAC ex = BER × SCF (exponential, 27.4, 12 driving cycles) vs COPERT E_TOTAL = E_HOT + E_COLD + E_EVAP.
  • VAPI evaporative uses temperature + altitude only; exhaust uses temperature + humidity + altitude.
