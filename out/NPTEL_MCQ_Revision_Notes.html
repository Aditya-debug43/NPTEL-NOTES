Multimodal transport L2·s33–35

  • Combining multiple modes to transport people/goods effectively from source to destination; also called "Combined transport" or "Intermodal transport".
  • Ensures seamless travel by: Vehicle mode integration (car, BRTS, metro etc.); Service integration (private & public modes); Infrastructure integration (bus & metro stations, parking facilities etc.).
  • Concept (s34): Home → Access modes (walking, bicycle/rickshaws, 2 wheeler, bus) → transfer stations (Car/Bus/Metro/BRTS/MRTS) → Egress modes (walking, bicycle/rickshaws, 2 wheeler, bus) → Office.
  • Challenges (s35): poor infrastructure facilities; intermodal linkage at regional and local levels; current aged transport modes; enhance ICT; modernization of public passenger terminals; role of Government to facilitate MMT.

Conclusions L2·s36

  • Transportation systems include all modes (road, rail, air, waterway, pipeline and cable) with infrastructure.
  • Efficient system = efficient policy measures + good infrastructure + integrated modes + integration with ICT to tackle congestion, pollution, negative externalities. Sustainable solutions = the future.

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Efficiency factors (11, Kadiyali) vs Development factors (5 groups × 4 scales, Rodrigue) vs Components (Sussman).
  • Frequency (how often) vs Regularity (punctuality/adhering to schedule) vs Adequacy (capacity).
  • Stakeholders are NOT customers or suppliers; Financial community = equity + debt.
  • Competition: intra-modal vs inter-modal (rail vs truck).

Lecture 3 — Concept of Sustainability (45 slides)

Why present development may be unsustainable L3·s3–6

  • Challenges since the Industrial Revolution; industrialization contributed to population growth, employment generation, wealth creation. Pollution = essential by-product of the present fossil-fuel based economic system. Some thought leaders: development is at the cost of future generations → unsustainable.
  • Factors contributing to unsustainable development (s4): polluting industrialization; increasing economy & per capita income with huge gap between rich and poor; unchecked population growth; continuous depletion of non-renewable resources; climate change.
  • Negative externalities (s5), 14 listed: air pollution; noise pollution; accidents; global warming; depletion of energy resources; consumption of land; loss of habitat; hazardous materials; vibration; visual intrusion; waste disposal; water pollution; congestion; social disruption.
  • Impacts (s6): food shortage; ecological crisis; slowing economic growth; raising social unrest; energy crisis; environmental pollution.

Definitions L3·s7–10

  • Sustainable development (Brundtland report, 1987): "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."
  • Development (Per Becker, 2014): preferred expected scenario of change in a set of variables over time, from a current to a desired state, including purposeful activity.
  • Negative events/processes may cause deviations from the preferred scenario. Sustainable development = development that can be maintained over time and safeguarded from impact of negative events.
  • Anticipating potential deviations (risk scenarios) is a requisite for sustainable development.
  • Evolution of the term (s11): Embryonic period (before 1972); Molding period (1972–1987); Developing period (after 1987).

Lecture 4 — Current Scenario of Transportation in India (44 slides)

Urban population & problems L4·s3–13

  • 2011 census: 31.2% of India's population (377 million) urban. UN estimates: 40% (590 million) by 2030; 58% (875 million) by 2050. About 63% of GDP contributed by the ~30% urban population. Scope for PURA (Shri APJ Abdul Kalam).
  • Transport problems (s4): growth in private vehicles; severe congestion; increasing GHG emissions; increasing road accidents; deteriorating air quality.
  • Private vehicle growth (s5): average annual growth rate 10%; private vehicle ownership increased to 438% in 2019 vs base year 2001.
  • Share of motorized vehicles (2011) (s6): two-wheelers have most share (about 75%) followed by private cars; two-wheeler ownership doubled in 2020 vs 2011.
  • Registered motor vehicles (s7, MORTH 2016): total increased from 0.3 million (1951) to 230.03 million (2016).

  • Infrastructure gap (s10): registered vehicle increase in last decade 219%; urban road infrastructure increase 124%. Rapid motorisation → severe congestion, longer journeys, higher per capita trips.
  • Congestion (s11): 3 Indian cities in top 10 congested cities: Mumbai 2nd, Bengaluru 6th, Delhi 8th, Pune 16th among 416 cities in 56 countries.

  • Potholes (s13): over 4770 accidents in 2019; accidents decreased but deaths due to potholes increased significantly in 2019.

Air pollution / GHG L4·s14–20

  • WHO 2018: 4.2 million deaths/year worldwide from ambient air pollution. 2017 Global Burden of Disease: ~1.1 million premature deaths/year in India; fifth leading cause of death.
  • GHG by sector in India (WRI CAIT 2018, s15): Energy 68.7%; Agriculture 19.6%; Industries 6%; Land-use change & forestry 3.8%; Waste 1.9%.
  • Road transportation > 90% of GHG from transportation sector in India (2014, MoEFCC) (s16).
  • G20 (s19): Turkey transport emissions per capita increased 38% (2013–2018); India and China 2nd and 3rd largest contributors; Saudi Arabia reduced by 25% (2013–2018).
  • Transportation sources ≈ one third of PM pollution in India (s20).
  • s17 (GHG from transport worldwide 2018) and s18 (per-capita emissions G20 excl. aviation, 2018) are charts (image).

Public transport & policy L4·s21–29

  • Public transport share only 18.1% of total work trips; 2015: 35 million daily motorcycle commuting trips (excl. personal); more people use motorcycles than bus (22.9 million) (s21).
  • National Urban Transport Policy (NUTP), 2006 — GOI policy framework for sustainable mobility (s22).
  • Smart cities (s23): two flagship programmes in 2015: 100 Smart Cities and AMRUT (Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation) for 500 cities with population ≥ 100,000; funding $8 billion and $8.3 billion respectively. Smart cities focus on core infrastructure service; AMRUT adopts a project approach for basic infrastructure services.
  • Metro (s25): Delhi Metro = largest, 213 km, 2.8 million passengers/day. MoUD: ~316 km in operation; >500 km under construction.
  • City bus (s26–27): >1.6 million buses registered; public bus sector operates 170,000 buses carrying ~70 million people/day. NURM launched 2009. BRT: ~222 km operational by 2015, remaining 282 km under construction; MoUD financial assistance to 11 cities for 504 km BRTS.
  • Future bus (s28–29): full indigenous retro-fitted electric buses with hybrid technology; hindrance = financial implication/capital cost; hybrid bus INR 23 million vs premium diesel bus INR 9 million. BMTC (Bengaluru) = first in India to launch an open-loop EMV contactless smartcard (not integrated with other modes). MMRDA appointed London's TfL to prepare integrated ticketing plan for Mumbai Metropolitan Region.

Non-motorized transport (NMT) L4·s30–35

  • NMT in India = walking, cycling, cycle rickshaws. Green mode: low carbon footprint, minimal energy consumption, zero local emissions, inexpensive, health benefits; low-income households largely depend on NMT; still neglected in favour of capital- and infrastructure-intensive modes.
  • NMT share (walking+cycling) 40–60% in 1980s; declining since 1980s except e.g. Chennai and Patna (s31).
  • Bicycle trips 10–30% of total trips; declined 22% in Kanpur by 1995, compared to 58% in Delhi and 86% in Jaipur; Patna increased 83% between 1990s and 2000s and again by 6% by 2008 (s32). [Slide numbers 83/86/58/58 appear scrambled in the graph; values as read.]
  • NMT blamed for congestion; rickshaws considerably increase road capacity on segregated lanes; 24% of Delhi metro trips depend on rickshaws as feeder (s33).
  • MoUD policy (s34–35): NUTP vision: NMT = last-mile connector and for short independent trips. First two principles of National Mission for Sustainable Habitat (PM's National Action Plan on Climate Change) = "walk" and "cycle". MoUD developed Service Level Benchmarks (SLBs). JNNURM 2005 required NMT as integral component. 2006 NUTP emphasised safe NMT infrastructure. BUT: NO DIRECT PROJECTS aimed exclusively at NMTs in India.

Lecture 5 — Climate Change: Indicators and Impacts (44 slides)

Basics L5·s3–8

  • Climate change: change in usual weather found in a place (e.g. rain per year, usual temperature for a month/season); also change in Earth's climate (usual temperature; places where rain and snow fall). Weather can change in a few hours; climate takes hundreds or even millions of years to change. (NASA 2014)
  • Causes (s4): Natural (not controllable): Earth's distance from the sun; oceans, volcano eruptions etc. Humans (controllable): anthropogenic activities; GHG emissions.
  • Is warming manmade? (s5): solar energy received followed the Sun's natural 11-year cycle with no net increase since the 1950s, while global temperature rose markedly → "extremely unlikely that the Sun has caused" the warming trend of past half-century.
  • s6: yearly temperature vs twentieth-century average 1880–2019 (red/blue bars) and atmospheric CO2 1880–2019 (grey line). s7: average global temperature & world carbon emissions from fossil fuel burning (millions of tons) 1800–2014.
  • Warmest years (s8): 2020 = 2nd warmest on record; 2016 warmest; 2019 3rd warmest; world's 7 warmest years all since 2014; 10 warmest all since 2005.

Lecture 6 — Impacts of Transportation Systems-I (30 slides)

Transport–environment link L6·s3–7

  • Transportation and environment = paradoxical: substantial socio-economic benefits but impacts environmental systems ("paradox of mobility and its costs").
  • Level of contribution: transport contributes (with other anthropogenic and natural causes) directly, indirectly, and cumulatively.
  • Scale of impact: local (noise and CO emissions) → global (climate change); also continental/national/regional (smog and acid rain).
  • Activities affecting the environment (s6): Infrastructure (resources, energy, land); Vehicle manufacture (resources & energy consumed); Vehicle operation (fossil fuel consumption); Vehicle maintenance (consumption & disposal of parts, lubricants); Vehicle disposal.

Energy L6·s8–13

  • Transport = about 20–25% of world energy use; strong correlation with level of development; 87% of world's total power production from fossil fuels.
  • World energy consumption (s10): Coal 39% (1965) → 28% (2016); Oil 40% (1965) → 33% (2016); Natural gas 15% (1965) → 24% (2016); Renewables 3% (2016).
  • Transport sector energy share (OECD, s11): 24.2% (1971) → 33.7% (2016).
  • Four major factors of energy use by transportation (s12): Vehicles (fuel efficiency); Infrastructure (transport efficiency); Demand (economic activity); Spatial infrastructure (average transport distance).
  • Fuel type (s13): oil-related fuels = 94%. Gasoline 43.5% most used; diesel 34.5%; heavy oil, biofuels, kerosene 21.8% [as written]; electricity and gases (e.g. LNG).

Emissions L6·s14–17

  • Energy generation = most significant contributor to global GHG; transport = 22% of global GHG emissions.
  • CO2 sharp increase after 1960s; China and India became the world's largest carbon emitters in early 21st century.
  • Global mean sea level (s17): main factors: increase in quantity of water in oceans; increase in global average temperatures. Increased risk of flooding & damage to coastal infrastructure.

Air quality & health L6·s19–22

  • Transport linked to climate change and air quality due to growing motorization and congestion.
  • Sources: highway vehicles, marine engines, locomotives, aircraft (gas + particulate emissions). Common pollutants: lead (Pb), carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen oxides (NOx), silicon tetrafluoride (SF6) [as written], benzene and volatile components (BTX), heavy metals (zinc, chrome, copper, cadmium), particulate matter (ash, dust).
  • Impact types (s22): Direct (noise pollution; CO emission); Indirect (secondary/tertiary) – higher consequence than direct (e.g. land clearing; particulates from incomplete combustion; respiratory & cardiovascular problems); Cumulative – additive, multiplicative or synergic (e.g. climate change).

Transport ↔ climate L6·s23–27

  • Fossil-fuel vehicles (cars, buses, 2 & 3 wheelers, steam trains, boats) → CO2 → greenhouse effect. Earth's average temperature up 0.8 °C since early 20th century [slide: "0.80C"].
  • s24: impacts of climate change on transport operations and infrastructure (figure). s25: carbon cycle – carbon pools in GtC; red = human emissions (figure).
  • Emissions by mode (s26): walking and cycling = best; sharing a vehicle reduces emissions vs single trips; mass/public transport has fewer emissions per trip.
  • Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) (s27): early stages of economic growth → pollution and degradation increase; beyond some level of income per capita the trend reverses → at high income, growth leads to environmental improvement (example fig: sulfur emissions).

Conclusion — GHG reduction measures L6·s28

  • Reduced use of private automobiles · increased use of public transport · alternate clean fuels · promotion of NMT · integrated transport development.

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Impact types: direct vs indirect (higher consequence) vs cumulative (additive/multiplicative/synergic).
  • Scale: local (noise, CO) vs regional (smog, acid rain) vs global (climate change).
  • Transport = 20–25% of world energy use vs 22% of global GHG vs 33.7% of OECD energy (2016).

Lecture 7 — Impacts of Transportation Systems-II: Human Health (49 slides)

Transport and health L7·s3–10

  • Transport negatively impacts health in three broad ways: Traffic accidents; Environmental pollution; Spread of infectious diseases. (K. J. Button 2010)
  • 1989: International Agency for Research on Cancer concluded diesel and gasoline engine exhausts are probably carcinogenic to humans. 1998: California Air Resources Board identified diesel PM as a toxic air contaminant (TAC). Children near heavily travelled roads: ~50% higher risk of respiratory symptoms from ambient PM10.
  • Private motorcars → reduced physical activity; lack of physical activity = major risk for coronary heart disease (leading cause of mortality in Europe). Walking & cycling: promote health; decrease noise and air pollution.
  • s6 framework (Khreis 2017): Urban transport policy & planning (built environment; transport provision & mode choice) → motor vehicle crashes, air pollution, noise, heat islands, lack of green space and biodiversity loss, physical inactivity, social exclusion, community severance, climate change → premature mortality / morbidity.
  • Mental health (s8–10): improving walking, cycling, public transit improves mental health via increased physical activity and community cohesion. Increased walkability reduces symptoms of depression in older men and frequency of dementia in women and men. High-quality public transport reduces commute stress vs driving. Children able to play unhindered by traffic found socially active twice as much; lack hinders independence, linked to depression. Effects: risk-taking and aggressive behaviours; depression; post-traumatic effects of accidents; high traffic → social isolation (higher mortality and morbidity in elderly).

Accidents L7·s11–12

  • Globally every year >1.17 million die in road crashes; >10 million crippled or injured. ~70% involve young people. India ≈ 6% of global incidents. ~45,000 deaths in the US in 2006.
  • Among developed countries, per capita traffic fatalities increase with per capita vehicle travel; United States has highest per capita crash rate (OECD).

Traffic-generated air pollution L7·s13–16, 27–31

  • Motor vehicle traffic = main source of ground-level urban concentrations of hazardous air pollutants. CO and benzene inside cars ≈ 2–5 times higher than at roadside. Car users more exposed than pedestrians, cyclists, public transport users.
  • Main pollutants: PM (PM10, PM2.5); CO; NOx; NMVOCs; O3; benzene. Trace: CH4, CO2, SOx, TSPs.
  • Short-term PM10 effects: increased mortality; respiratory & cardiovascular diseases; more respiratory symptoms and medication use by asthmatics; reduced lung function. Recurrent cumulative exposure ↑ morbidity, ↓ life expectancy.
  • Determinants (s27): number of vehicles; age of vehicles; engine type & condition; type of fuel; meteorology; shape of urban environment; traffic organization.
  • Heavy vehicles = major polluters: diesel truck produces 50–100 times more fine and ultra-fine particles/km than a car with catalytic converter. Modern diesel emits less PM2.5 but more ultra-fine particles; ultra-fine particles (number rather than mass) suspected main cause of health effects → new diesels may cause more harm.
  • Health end-points (s29): mortality (long term); cardiovascular; respiratory; acute bronchitis (<15 y); asthma exacerbation (<15 y and ≥15 y); restricted physical activity (15–64 y); lung cancer.
  • Fine particulate pollution responsible for most of the 3–4 million annual air-pollution deaths worldwide (s31).

Los Angeles case L7·s17–26

  • People in LA county exposed to 60% more vehicular pollution than state average. Thick smog from the 1940s, lasted over 3 decades; LA = "City of Stars" also "City of Smog"; 1964/1966 photos (gas masks, low visibility, accidents). Clean Air Act passed 1970 → harsher control measures. LA used to be the most polluted air basin in US.

Indirect impacts L8·s32–35 (World Bank 1997)

Lecture 9 — Impacts of Transportation Systems-IV: Soil, Water, Acid Rain (53 slides)

Soil and water — physical impacts L9·s3–5

  • Construction clears, levels, fills, cuts natural material → changes soil density, landscape relief, surface and groundwater flows, microclimate → alters land cover, vegetation, habitat composition.
  • Wetlands & habitats especially sensitive to hydrology change (embankments, cuttings may drain aquifers, ↑ soil erosion, earth slides); can pollute watercourses with sediments; canalisation of surface water into ditches changes run-off and debris flows.

Chemical impacts L9·s6–8

  • Pollutants: road dust, salt, heavy metals, fertiliser nutrients, toxins. Accumulate near infrastructure but effects on vegetation/fauna seen at distances of several hundreds of metres.
  • De-icing salts (NaCl, CaCl2, KCl, MgCl2): damage vegetation, contaminate drinking water, reduce soil pH → increases mobility of heavy metals.
  • Heavy/trace metals (Pb, Zn, Cu, Cr, Cd, Al from petrol, de-icing salts, dust) accumulate in plant/animal tissues; affect reproduction & survival.
  • Exhaust toxins: PAH, dioxins, ozone, nitrogen, CO2, fertilising chemicals. Emissions in lakes & heathlands at >200 m from road can change plant growth and species diversity.

Water quality — roads L9·s9–11

  • Road drainage & excavation lower the water table; embankments & structures raise it by restricting flow. Can contribute to flooding, soil erosion, channel modification, siltation of streams. Road construction blocks subsurface streams → wetting upflow side and drying downflow side. Improper/inadequate drainage → rising water tables or flooding.

Water transport impacts L9·s12–20

  • Marine transport emissions = most important segment of water-quality impact of transportation sector. Main effects from: dredging, waste, ballast waters, oil spills.
  • Dredging: deepening harbor channels by removing sediments from the bed of a water body. Waste has very high bacteria level – hazardous to public health and marine ecosystems.
  • Vessel emissions: air pollutants; water emissions – cargo hold cleaning water, ballast water, grey water, sewage, oil spills.
  • Ballast water discharge from other parts of the world introduces invasive species → domestic species may go extinct (pollution different from emission type).
  • Oil spills: mostly ship pollution; less frequent than daily-operation pollution but devastating; toxic to marine life. PAHs (components of crude oil) hard to clean, last years in sediment; cause developmental problems, disease susceptibility, abnormal reproductive cycles.

Acid rain L9·s23–29

  • Formed when acidic pollutants SO2 and NO2 dissolve in rain water.
  • SO2(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ H2SO3 (sulphurous acid) → with O2 slowly oxidized to H2SO4 (sulphuric acid): H2SO3 + O2 → H2SO4 [as written]. NO2 (with O2, H2O): 4NO2 + 2H2O → 4HNO3 [as written] (nitric acid).
  • Pathway: 1. SO2/NOx emitted → 2. transformed into acid particles, transported long distances → 3. fall as wet and dry deposition (dust, rain, snow) → 4. harm soil, forests, streams, lakes.

Mitigation L9·s31–42 (World Bank 2019)

  • Good road drainage features: cutoff/catch-water drain, scour checks, culvert, natural water course, side drain, turn-out drains (s31). Storm water inlets & sewers remove water from road surfaces (s32); highway surface drainage on sides and middle (s33).
  • Infiltration: cross and side drains → groundwater recharge structures (s34); roadside infiltration trenches with bunds intercept surface runoff (s35); tube recharge wells = Bhungroo in Gujarat, India (s36); road water harvesting to surface storage (s38).
  • Irrigation from road drainage (s37): runoff directed to interconnected soaking pits; upper bund breached then lower bund; repeat until all fields watered; smooth water movement to avoid erosion; fields well levelled. Examples: rice drainage via roadside borrow pit – Bangladesh (s39); grapes with road water – Bolivia (s40); collecting road water – Shandong, China (s41); urban lake "Ijzeren Vrouw" from borrow pit – Netherlands (s42).

Noise and its effects L10·s3–8

  • Noise = unwanted sound; level of sound exceeding acceptable level and creating annoyance. Main contributors: urbanization and traffic. Noise from vehicles ≈ 70% of total traffic noise. Sources: road & rail traffic, aircraft, industry, construction.
  • Effects in three groups: Subjective, Behavioral, Physiological.
GroupDescription
SubjectiveAnnoyance, disturbance, noisiness; difficult to measure precisely (people perceive differently, e.g. music vs noise).
BehavioralInterference with speech, sleep or other tasks; sleep disturbance in dense traffic areas, near railway tracks or airports.
PhysiologicalSudden shock/frightening effect; headache; extremely high noise for long duration → deafness.
  • Harmful effects on humans (s8): interferes with speech; emotional & behavioral stress; permanent hearing loss; diseases (headache, blood pressure, heart failure); health effects (increased heart beat, constriction of blood vessels, dilation of pupil); problematic for patients needing rest; potential to damage liver, brain and heart.

Factors & sources of vehicular noise L10·s11–20

  • Factors affecting traffic noise are grouped: Traffic factors, Road factors, Vehicle factors, Human factors (s11–16; contents are images). Human factors: irresponsible/inappropriate behavior; 90% of accidents linked to human error (s17).
  • Facts (s13): motor vehicles noisier than passenger cars [as written]; diesel engines noisier than petrol; commercial trucks & loaded vehicles noisier than other road modes; noise directly proportional to age of vehicle (older → more noise).
  • Two major vehicle sources (s18): Power plant & transmission noise (engine, exhaust, intake, cooling system); Running gear noise (tyre–road interaction, differential, propeller shaft). On highways: at lower speeds engine-exhaust noise dominant; at higher speeds road–tyre interaction dominant.

Noise measurement L10·s21–30

  • Sound level meter block chain (s21): microphone → transducer signal → pre-amplifier / filters → weighing network → amplifier → rectifier → averaging system → display (or AC outlet).
  • Acoustic arrays (s22): number of microphones in various patterns; omnidirectional; receive sound from wide area.
  • Decibel (s23): SPL (Lp) = logarithmic measure of effective pressure relative to reference; unit dB. Lp = ln(p/p0) Np = 2 log10(p/p0) B = 20 log10(p/p0) dB; p = rms sound pressure; p0 = reference. 1 B = 10 dB. Reference in air p0 = 20 µPa = threshold of human hearing (≈ mosquito flying 3 m away).
  • Leq (equivalent continuous noise level) (s24–26): formula (s26): Leq = 10 log[ (1/(t2−t1)) ∫ (pA²/p0²) dt ] from t1 to t2 (pA = acquired sound pressure; p0 = 20 µPa). preferred method for time-varying sound; single dB value accounting for total sound energy over time. Example: over 24 h as low as 30 dB(A) at night, 70 dB(A) with occasional vehicles, higher by day. Meter stores fluctuations, calculates "average energy" – not arithmetic average: dB converted to SPL, summed, divided by number of samples, converted back to dB.
  • Energy average example (s27): levels 88, 93 [text] and 97 dBA: arithmetic mean = (88+94+97)/3 = 93 dBA; energy-average Leq = 10 log10{[108.8+109.4+109.7]/3} = 94.3 dBA. [Slide text writes "93" and "94" inconsistently; kept as on slide.]
  • A-weighting dB(A) (s28–29): most common weighting; cuts off lower and higher frequencies average person cannot hear; imitates human ear; ear responds more to 500 Hz–8 kHz, less sensitive to very low/high pitch. Units dBA or dB(A).
  • n-percent exceeded level Ln (s30): sound level exceeded n% of time. L10 > L50 > L90. L10: exceeded 10% of time – sporadic/intermittent events. L50: 50% – median / fluctuating noise. L90: 90% – background/ambient noise.

Mitigating measures L10·s31–33

  • Green plantations (bushes/trees around sources) · Regular servicing & tuning of automobiles · Green buildings (noise-absorbing materials for walls, windows, ceilings) · Wearables (ear plugs, earmuffs) · Lubrication & regular servicing of machinery · Policy regulations (restrict loudspeakers in crowded/public areas) · Soundproof doors/windows · Land use (factories far from residential areas) · Community development programs (long-term planning) · Social awareness programs.

Noise barriers L10·s34–46

  • Barriers reduce sound by blocking straight-line path source→receiver; reduction ≈ 5 dB; further 3 dB by making the barrier much higher (longer path) → combined 8 dB = equivalent to reducing traffic by factor of 6. Diffraction depends on ratio of barrier height h to wavelength λ; higher barrier → more efficient.
  • Heights (s36): min permissible 1–2.5 m; max 4–10 m or more; average (most common) 2–5 m. Shadow zone behind barrier.

  • Transmission loss: sound level reduced when forced to travel through the barrier; 30 dB transmission loss = practically all (99.9%) sound blocked.
  • Insertion loss: difference in sound level without barrier vs after barrier installed between source and receiver.
  • Insertion loss (IL), Kurze & Anderson model (s46): IL = 5 dB + 20 log[ √(2πN) / tanh√(2πN) ] dB for N up to 12.5; IL = 20 dB for N > 12.5. N = Fresnel number = non-dimensional measure of how much farther the sound must travel to reach the receiver due to the barrier; N = (a + b − l)·f / C0; l = original direct path length source→receiver; a, b = the two straight segments of the path modified by the barrier; f = sound frequency (Hz); C0 = speed of sound in air (approx. 1125 ft/sec or 343 m/s).
  • Numerical problem (s47–48): 12 ft barrier, 12 ft from vehicle; house 15 ft beyond barrier; window height 4 ft. l = √(27²+4²) = 27.3 ft; a = √(12²+12²) = 17 ft; b = √(15²+8²) = 17 ft; a+b−l = 34 − 27.3 = 6.7 ft. At f = 100 Hz, N = (6.7 × 100)/1100 = 0.61 [slide uses 1100]; IL = 5 + 20 log[√(2π×0.61)/tanh√(2π×0.61)] ≈ 10 dB.
  • Conclusion (s49): loud, regular exposure → effects from slight disturbance to even death; reduce noise from planning stage; trees/shrubs and barriers help.

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Subjective (annoyance) vs Behavioral (speech/sleep interference) vs Physiological (shock, headache, deafness).
  • Leq = energy average, not arithmetic mean (94.3 vs 93 dBA). L10 (intermittent) > L50 (median) > L90 (background).
  • Transmission loss (through barrier; 30 dB = 99.9% blocked) vs insertion loss (with vs without barrier).
  • Low speeds: engine-exhaust noise dominates; high speeds: tyre–road dominates. Diesel > petrol; older vehicle = noisier.
  • Barrier: 5 dB + 3 dB = 8 dB ≈ traffic ÷ 6. Reference pressure 20 µPa; 1 B = 10 dB.
