L40 — Modelling of Transport Emissions-I

Vehicular emissions & inventory s3–8

  • Three types of vehicular emissions: Exhaust, Evaporative, Non-exhaust (tyre and brake).
  • Exhaust: CO, HC, NOx, CO₂, PM, benzene, 1-3 butadiene, formaldehyde, PAH (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons), acetaldehyde, total aldehyde, GHG emissions. Evaporative: VOCs/hydrocarbons. Non-exhaust: tyre & brake emissions – PM2.5 and PM10, heavy metals.
  • Emission inventory = important tool to assess vehicular emissions based on emission factors, average speed, fuel consumption and amount of traffic on a defined road type; developed manually or by computer-based models. Models developed on: vehicle type; fuel category & engine technology; vehicle kilometers travelled (VKT); emission standards; various correction factors.
  • Decision Support System (DSS) framework: model-based emission inventory development → data analysis/action to implement policy → traffic development & policy implementation → evaluation of efficiency of policies. Emission model = summation of product of emissions from all vehicular sources, vehicular activities and climatic & topographic variables.
  • Model inputs: vehicle population, type, age, model, technology, fuel type, VKT, temperature, humidity, altitude. Outputs: emission inventory by pollutants; vehicle-type contribution; efficiency of current policy; input data for dispersion models; vehicular emission trends; fuel consumption & contribution.

Classification of transportation emission models s9–10

Static (Top-Down, macro-scale) = aggregated emission factor models, average speed models. Dynamic (Bottom-Up, micro-scale) = traffic situation models, instantaneous models. Top-down works on macro scale only; bottom-up uses detailed micro-level data.

Vehicular emission models s11–38

Examples (s11): MOBILE series (USEPA); MOVES (USEPA); EMFAC (CARB); COPERT (EEA); QGEPA model (Australia); IVE model (funded by USEPA). s12 diagram: road vehicles → movement of goods/passengers; fuel in; evaporative & exhaust emissions; road-vehicle tyre & brake wear; road wear.

ModelKey facts
MOBILE (USEPA) s13–15Highway-vehicle emissions in the U.S. except California; MOBILE6.2 last version (2004), later updated to/superseded by MOVES; calculates HC, NOx, CO from cars, light & heavy duty vehicles; uses ambient temperature, humidity, altitude; basic emission rates for average speed of 31.5 km/h for light-duty gasoline vehicles by model year & technology; computes hot running emissions per unit distance for 5 mile/hr speed increments: ex = SCF (BER + EO) – SCF speed correction factor, BER basic emission rate, EO emission offset (correction for "off-cycle" emissions – additional emissions due to high power operation not in BER)
MOVES (MOtor Vehicle Emission Simulator) s16–18Currently state-of-the-science emission system of USEPA; mobile sources across U.S. except California; air pollutants, GHGs, air toxics; MOVES3 latest (after MOVES2010, MOVES2014); emission types: engine running, starting, hoteling (extended idling), evaporative, brake & tyre; inputs: default national averages – vehicle counts, VMT (Vehicle Miles Travelled), temperature, fuel, location etc. Running-emission calculation inputs: vehicle miles travelled, average speed, driving cycle, source hours operating, operating mode distribution, emission rates (g/hr), adjustments (fuel parameters, temperature, humidity, A/C) → adjusted emissions; summed by model year, regulatory class, fuel type
EMFAC (California Air Resources Board, CARB) s19–23Emission inventories of on-road mobile sources in California; input from CALIMFAC (California Motor Vehicle Emissions Factor Model: emission rates and weight for vehicle activity by model year); vehicle characteristics (VKT, starts, number of vehicles) provided by BURDEN model; latest EMFAC2021. Equation: ex = BER × SCF = BER × EXP(b1(v−27.4) + b2(v−27.4)²); BER = mean emission factor of individual vehicle (total g in lab test, unified cycle length in mile); SCF developed from 12 driving cycles; b1, b2 model parameters; v in mile/h. EMFAC2021 vs MOVES3 evaporative emissions (passenger cars, Los Angeles county): similar trends & magnitudes; EMFAC2021 uses California-specific inputs. NOx start emissions (heavy-duty diesel trucks): EMFAC2021 higher than EMFAC2017 especially for soak times < 400 minutes. (Soak time = duration engine is off [slide text cut off].)
COPERT (European Environment Agency, EEA) s24–29Developed by EEA; CORINAIR working group (first European initiative on road-traffic emissions) transformed to COPERT in 1989; COPERT 5.4.52 latest (May 2021); computes hot running, evaporative, non-exhaust (brakes, tyres); 105 vehicle categories in 5 classes (cars, light & heavy duty vehicles, urban buses & coaches, two wheelers); pollutants: CO, NOx, VOC, CH₄, CO₂, N₂O, NH₃, SOx, diesel PM, PAHs, POPs, dioxins, furans, heavy metals (lead, cadmium, copper, chromium, nickel, selenium, zinc). E_TOTAL = E_HOT + E_COLD + E_EVAP; also E_TOTAL = E_URBAN + E_RURAL + E_HIGHWAY (driving conditions); E_EVAP only relevant for NMVOC from gasoline vehicles; E_COLD = transient thermal engine operation (cold start). New in 5.4: plug-in hybrid (PHEV) passenger cars; hybrid buses; revised Euro 5 & Euro 6 factors for passenger cars & LCVs; parameter updates/error fixes. PHEV in charge-sustaining (CS) mode: petrol PHEV → equation for petrol hybrid; diesel PHEV → equation for diesel conventional
QGEPA (Queensland Govt Environmental Protection Agency, Australia) s30–31Travel-speed emission model; hot running emission factor database for CO, HC, PM, NOx in g/km; classes: light duty (petrol, diesel, LPG), heavy duty (diesel, CNG, LPG), motorcycles; separate factors for cold start and evaporative; also non-regulated pollutants (hydrocarbons, N₂O, NH₃, PAH); speed correction factors used for evaporative running loss emissions for cold start
IVE (International Vehicle Emissions) s32–38Developed jointly by UC Riverside CE-CERT, GSSR, ISSRC; funded by USEPA; accounts for technologies & conditions in developing countries (driving patterns, vehicle specific power (VSP), engine stress distributions); inputs: type & age of fleet, local conditions (temperature, pressure), fuel specifications; estimates criteria pollutants, GHGs, toxics: CO, VOC, NOx, PM2.5, PM10, CO₂, N₂O, CH₄, NH₃, benzene, lead, 1,3-butadiene, aldehydes; emissions from a single roadway or entire area for specific time periods. Two basic equations: Eq. 1 Q_T = B(t) × K(base) × K(temp) × K(Hmd) × K(IM) × K(Alt) × K(Cntry) – adjusted emission rate = base rates × correction factors; Eq. 2 Q_running = U[FTP] × D/Uc × Σ_t{f[t] × Q[t] × Σ_d{f[dt] × K[dt]}} – weights adjusted rate by travel fraction per technology & driving type; final step multiplies by ratio of average velocity of LA-4 driving cycle to average velocity of modeled cycle and by distance (running emissions only); result = overall fleet running emissions (g). U.S. FTP-72 cycle also called UDDS or LA-4 cycle. Variables: B[t] base rate; f[t] fraction of travel by technology; f[dt] fraction of each driving type; K(Tmp), K(Hmd), K(Alt), K(Fuel), K(IM) inspection/maintenance, K(Cntry), K[dt] driving correction factor; D distance/day (km)

Indian context s39–42

  • Pune example: dominant vehicles two-wheelers followed by three-wheelers; buses and light-duty four-wheelers major NOx source.
  • Model complexity & errors: reliability depends on accuracy and robustness; accuracy improved by more complex formulations, but robustness tends to be negated by increasing input errors.
  • Limitations for India: MOBILE, MOVES, COPERT etc. developed in developed countries (US, Japan, Australia) with extensive datasets; differences in technology, infrastructure, driving cycle → may give erroneous results; emission factors/basic emission rates are US & Europe based; IVE designed for developing countries like India but complexity similar to US/European models (extensive data, hard to compile); most models cannot give output for more than a year; need experimental data not available for India; most are region specific.

VAPI model (Vehicular Air Pollution Inventory, IIT Roorkee) s43–54

  • Designed specifically for developing countries like India; incorporates climatic correction factors; fair agreement with Indian air-quality observations; simple to use, public domain.
  • Assumptions: vehicles per 1000 persons grows in a Sigmoidal (S-shaped) relationship; saturation = one car plus two wheelers per family; seating capacities of auto-rickshaws & taxis 3 and 5 → saturation per 1000 persons 330 and 200. Vehicle population projection based on GDP and per capita income using the Gompertz model; on-road population = projected past-year population subtracted from cumulative registered population; base emission factors depend on vehicle age and model year.
  • Pollutants: running exhaust – CO, HC, NOx, CO₂, PM, benzene, 1-3 butadiene, formaldehyde, total PAH, acetaldehyde, total aldehyde; running loss evaporative emissions (VOC); non-exhaust (brakes, tyres) – PM10 & PM2.5.
  • Running exhaust: EF_a,p,i = EF_p,i × C_T × C_H × C_A (temperature, humidity, altitude correction factors); E_t,p,i = P_i × EF_a,p,i × V_i × D_t,i (P = on-road population; V = per day distance km/day; D = annual travelling days). Evaporative: EF_a = EF × C_T × C_A (temperature & altitude only); running losses from petrol vehicles are largest source of VOC evaporative emissions (others: diurnal, hot soak, resting losses); running losses = vapor generated in gasoline tanks during vehicle operation. Non-exhaust PM: generated mechanically by abrasion of tyre, brake, road pavement and resuspension of road dust.

Conclusion s55

  • Models mainly developed by US, Europe, Australia; complex, predict emissions of all vehicle categories; but mostly country specific and based on extensive native datasets; VAPI is a simple model designed for developing countries like India.
Important Distinctions to Remember (L40)
  • Static = top-down = macro (aggregate emission factor, average speed) vs dynamic = bottom-up = micro (traffic situation, instantaneous).
  • MOBILE (superseded; US except California) → MOVES (current; US except California) vs EMFAC (California) vs COPERT (EEA, Europe) vs QGEPA (Queensland, Australia) vs IVE (developing countries; UC Riverside etc.; USEPA-funded) vs VAPI (IIT Roorkee, India).
  • MOBILE ex = SCF (BER + EO) vs EMFAC ex = BER × SCF (exponential, 27.4, 12 driving cycles) vs COPERT E_TOTAL = E_HOT + E_COLD + E_EVAP.
  • VAPI evaporative uses temperature + altitude only; exhaust uses temperature + humidity + altitude.
