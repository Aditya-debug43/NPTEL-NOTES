L37 — MFA Case Study: Vienna (Austria) Urban Transport, Projections to 2050

Study area, background, need s3–5

  • Vienna = capital and most populous city of Austria (≈ 2 million); robust public transport.
  • Background: in cities transport contributes significantly to global resource & energy consumption; 10–20% of Europe's urban population exposed to excessive NO₂ (highest along busy roads); decarbonizing transport is an important means of mitigating climate change; studies focused almost exclusively on energy and CO₂, neglecting consumption of material resources needed for low-carbon transformation.
  • Need: target to reduce Vienna's per capita CO₂-eq emissions in transport by 50% by 2030 and by 100% by 2050; propulsion technologies (EV) and shift to environment-friendly modes (walking, public transport); modal shift vs technological development are often counter-poles in decision-making → optimum scenario needed.

Objectives, research questions, system s6–11

  • Objectives: effects of transforming urban transport on future material stock and flows; MFA of infrastructure and vehicles until 2050 under different scenarios (characterized mainly by different modal splits); time span 2016 to 2050.
  • Research questions: how will infrastructure & vehicle fleet develop to 2050; how do developments affect material stocks & flows; are there saving potentials for raw materials and how can they be realized.
  • System boundary: transport system within administrative border of Vienna; infrastructure and vehicles; modes: MIT (motorized individual transport), NMIT (non-motorized individual transport; also written NMT), PT (public transport).
  • Materials (in metric tons t, Mt): asphalt & bitumen, aluminum, batteries, brickwork, concrete, copper, glass, gravel & sand, iron & steel, rubber, plastics, wood (12 groups).
  • Flows & stocks: construction & demolition of transport infrastructure; maintenance of infrastructure also considered; annual input/output flows computed from changes in stocks (increase/decrease); maintenance flows based on renewal rates and useful lives. Not included: material flows of vehicle maintenance and network operation.

Projections / results s16–27

  • Traffic modes: highest personal-vehicle trips in scenario B (cars 6,240 million vehicle-km/year in 2050 vs 5,470 in A; 1,680 in C and D); least in C & D; most PT in C. 2020→2030: no major improvement in NMT/PT share.
  • Vehicle share: drastic jump in EVs 2020→2030 & 2050; EVs also for commercial goods transport; 95% of motorbikes battery-based by 2050. [2050 cars (scenario row): petrol 3%, diesel 3%, battery-electric 82%, hydrogen 10%; lorry <3.5 t BEV 95%, >3.5 t 50% – read from table.]
  • Active mobility: share of bicycles reduced in B, C, D vs A and 2030 (replaced by pedelecs = e-bicycles); steady growth in 2050 scenarios except A.
  • Public transport: grows drastically in 2050 vs 2020 in best case; tram share of PT up to 47% in scenario D from 33% in 2020.
  • Private motorized vehicle: conventional vehicle use lower in 2030 than 2020; trips significantly reduced in C & D in 2050; people start walking/cycling for short distances with better PT & walking/cycle infrastructure.
  • Car fleet (B vs C): petrol & diesel fleet obsolete by 2050; 70% fewer vehicles in C compared to B; hybrid cars very few in both; in B, hydrogen vehicles increase rapidly after 2030.
  • Traffic area = sum of road, parking and walking/pedestrian areas; road & parking area reduced in C & D vs A & B; traffic area reduced with PT/active mobility.
  • Rail network length highest for C & D; same value for C and D affirms PT and NMT are supplementary to each other.
  • Transport construction material: travel demand rises with time → material inflow rises; scenario D least material usage; scenario B higher due to equal traffic area but increased material for vehicle manufacturing. Per-capita inflow: significant MIT-material reduction in C & D vs A & B.
  • Cumulative infrastructure in/outputs: flows similar for A & B, also for C & D; outputs of C & D higher than A & B; major shares: sand/gravel, concrete, asphalt, steel. Vehicles: major components steel, aluminum, plastics; output for C & D higher than input due to dismantling of existing road infrastructure [as stated].

Conclusions s28–31

  • Choice of transport modes & motorization rate strongly affect material stock & annual resource consumption; primary raw-material consumption reducible by shifting from private motorized vehicles to PT and active mobility.
  • If BAU retained, resource consumption & waste keep rising although GHG emissions might be mitigated (fewer fossil-fuel vehicles).
  • Greatest savings potential: reduction in private vehicle fleet and road infrastructure and associated lower maintenance.
  • To avoid negative feedback loops, obsolete (road) infrastructure should be dismantled and converted for other purposes; resource aspect should be considered in transport projects.
  • MFA can evaluate mode-share scenarios; also use MFA besides direct impact evaluation; promoting PT and NMT helps resource conservation.
Important Distinctions to Remember (L37)
  • Scenario keys: A = BAU; B = EV fleet (car share 25%, highest car trips & higher material); C = PT (PT 55%, MIT <10%, 70% fewer cars than B); D = Active mobility (NMT >45%, least material).
  • MIT vs NMIT/NMT vs PT. Pedelec = e-bicycle.
  • Maintenance of infrastructure included; vehicle maintenance & network operation not included.
