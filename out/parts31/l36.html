L36 — Material Flow Analysis (MFA)

Concept, definition, history s3–8

  • MFA is based on the law of mass conservation: matter can change form through physical & chemical changes but is conserved; mass going into a system = mass coming out + mass absorbed (stored) in the system. Law dates from Antoine Lavoisier's 1789 discovery (mass neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions).
  • MFA definition (Brunner & Rechberger, 2004): systematic assessment of the flows and stocks of materials within a system defined in space and time.
  • Equation: Input = Output + Stock. Example: 100 L fuel × 2 g lead/L = Input 200 g; Output 150 g lead in air; Stock = 200 − 150 = 50 g (remains within system).
  • History: conservation of matter (input = output) first postulated by Greek philosophers more than 2000 years ago; mass-balance principle applied in medicine, chemistry, economics, engineering, life sciences. Two original application areas: metabolism of cities; analysis of pollutant pathways in regions such as watersheds or urban areas.
  • Objectives (1/2, 2/2): delineate system of material flows & stocks; reduce system complexity while maintaining basis for decision-making; assess relevant flows & stocks quantitatively (check mass balance, sensitivities, uncertainties); present results in reproducible, understandable, transparent fashion; use results as basis for managing resources, environment, wastes; monitor accumulation/depletion of stocks, future environmental loadings; design environmentally-beneficial goods, processes, systems. Examples: hazardous substance causing health issues – create flow & stock of incoming/outgoing/stocking; PM10 vehicular emission – study environmental loading in a city region.

Procedure of MFA s14–16

  1. Identification of key (material flow related) issues
  2. System analysis (selection of relevant matter, processes, indicator substances (elements), and system boundaries)
  3. Quantification of mass flows of matter and indicator substances
  4. Identification of weak points in the system
  5. Development and evaluation of scenarios, representation, interpretation of results

[Order read from the colored chain diagram.] Flow chart (s15): problem definition → system definition (selection of substances, system boundaries, processes, goods) → determination of flows & stocks (mass flows; balancing of goods; concentrations; balancing of substances) → illustration & interpretation; feedback loops: refine mass flows, refine concentrations, redetermine goods, adjust system, redefine problem.

Applications s19–21

  • Environmental management: environmental impact statements; remediation of hazardous waste sites; design of air pollution control strategies; nutrient management in watersheds; planning of soil-monitoring systems. (Ex.: clear picture of pollutant output from thermal plant/region → mitigation strategies.)
  • Resource & waste management: analysis, planning, allocation, exploitation, upgrading of resources (ex.: limiting natural-resource exploitation, EV battery upgradation); modeling elemental compositions of wastes; evaluating material management performance in recycling/treatment facilities (output below threshold concentration of hazardous material).
  • Industrial ecology: controlling pathways for materials use & industrial processes; creating loop-closing industrial practices; dematerializing industrial output; systematizing patterns of energy use; balancing industrial input/output to natural ecosystem capacity.

Advantages & limitations s22–24

  • Advantages: critical view of resource management status in a city; evaluates environmental soundness of alternatives; decision tool to choose sustainable technology; ideal technical basis for planning & decision making especially in developing and emerging countries with limited technical/financial resources; suitable for early detection of environmental problems in developing countries.
  • Limitations: needs a lot of data (only limited reliable data for developing countries); must deal with uncertainties due to dynamic nature of processes.

Numerical example s25–27

10 km stretch of 2-way 2-lane road. Absorbing capacity: vegetation 0.8 t CO₂/10000 m²/h; road carriageway 0.2; concrete surface 0.2. Diagram labels: 10 km length; dimensions 180 m, 90 m, 70 m (which label refers to which width is not clear from the diagram). Traffic table (emission t CO₂/km; first lane/h; second lane/h): 2-wheelers 0.4 (10; 20); cars 1 (30; 15); small trucks 2 (15; 20); trucks 3 (5; 5); bicycle 0.1 (5; 5); e-cars 0.6 (1; 3).

  • Stock = absorption by surface = [0.2×(2×90) + 0.2×70 + 0.8×(2×10)] × 24 = 1584 ton.
  • Input = CO₂ emission from vehicular movement = (emission per km × 10 km × vehicles/hr × 24) = 1640 ton CO₂/day.
  • Output = Input − Stock = 1640 − 1584 = 56 ton CO₂/day.

MFA vs EIA vs LCA s28–29

MethodSlide statements
LCAStrives for completeness – as many substances as possible related to product; can be done for any specific product (e.g. aircraft, material used in construction)
MFAStrives for transparency and manageability – limited number of substances; can establish an inventory for an LCA (e.g. fuel, EV battery input/output); assesses inflow & outflow of material for project area (system boundary); doesn't analyse impacts
EIAImpact assessment of a project; generally considers impacts on local environment after implementation

Airport example: MFA studies input and outgoing materials; EIA assesses impacts of construction & operation activities.

s30 Conclusion: MFA assesses flow of materials within system; applications: environmental management, resource management, industrial ecology; compares alternatives → most resource-conservative option.

Important Distinctions to Remember (L36)
  • Flow = mass/time; flux = mass/time/area. Imports/exports = across system boundaries; inputs/outputs = across process boundaries.
  • Substance (single type of matter) vs goods (economic value) vs material (substances and goods). System vs activity vs stock.
  • MFA = transparency/manageability, no impact analysis; LCA = completeness; EIA = project impacts on local environment.
  • Stock = Input − Output.
