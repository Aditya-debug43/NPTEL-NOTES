L35 — LCA Case Study: LCA of Biomass-based Energy Systems, Denmark

Study area & background s3–6

  • Denmark: Northern Europe; capital Copenhagen (cycle-friendly infrastructure); high-income economy.
  • Target: 100% renewable energy system in 2050, primarily wind power and biomass, plus significant decrease in national energy demand. Realistic only through: significant reductions in energy demand; increased efficiency of fuel conversion technologies; higher share of wind power (e.g. up to 50%); replacement of fossil fuels with biomass; integration of transport sector into energy system (e.g. electric vehicles).
  • 2008 sector-wise final energy consumption (pie): Transport 34% (highest), Households 29%, Industry 18%, Services/Commerce 13%, Agriculture 6%. [Slide text says households & industry follow with 29 & 18%.]
  • 2008 energy sources: fossil fuels ≈ 84% of primary energy supply; only 16% renewable (biomass, solar, waste); share of oil 39%.

Scope, objectives, methodology s7–13

  • Scope: environmental consequences of changing energy production; all upstream, direct and downstream emissions via life-cycle perspective; quantify impacts of future scenarios for 2030 and 2050; compare with reference year 2008.
  • Objectives (3): identification of potential biomass resources; selection of suitable biomass conversion technologies & efficiencies; quantification of environmental impacts of selected energy scenarios.
  • Goal: assess environmental impacts of more sustainable energy scenarios; scenarios based on mix of residual agricultural resources, energy crops, wind & other renewables, and fossil fuels as needed; compare with 2008 reference.
  • Functional unit: "meeting the energy demand (electricity, heat and fuels) required in Denmark" for 2008, 2030, 2050; does not describe amount of energy but ensures future living standards same as 2008 baseline.
  • Scope set-up: LCA modelling for Primary Energy Supply (PES); results normalized with PES for each year; impacts quantified with time horizon of 100 years.
  • Assessment approach: select scenarios from national energy strategies & political targets → select conversion technologies & data → balance energy supply and demand per scenario → impact assessment & discussion.
  • Impact categories: global warming (CO₂ eq); land occupation; acidification (area of unprotected ecosystem per unit energy); aquatic eutrophication with two sub-categories – nitrogen & phosphorous.

Impact results s28–31 (charts)

  • Global warming (Gg CO₂-eq/PJ_PES): 2008 = 68; 2030 = 38; 2050 CSV = 20; RME = 31; BtL = 17. (PP power plants; LUC = dLUC + iLUC; BtE biomass-to-energy.)
  • Acidification (10⁶ m²/PJ_PES): 2008 = 301; 2030 = 193; CSV = 121; RME = 177; BtL = 135.
  • Eutrophication-N (Mg N/PJ_PES): 2008 = 14; 2030 = 16; CSV = 15; RME = 29; BtL = 24.
  • Additional land occupation (10⁶ Δm²/PJ_PES, vs 2008 base): 2030 = 624; CSV = 861; RME = 2089; BtL = 1787.

Study conclusions s32–35

  • Significant GHG/global-warming reductions achievable per PJ of energy supplied.
  • Residual domestic biomass was insufficient → extra cultivation of energy crops → significant environmental loads in most categories.
  • Large impacts of upstream land use changes (LUCs) made fossil diesel for heavy transport favorable to RME- and FT-biodiesel.
  • High aquatic eutrophication = direct consequence of energy-crop cultivation (increased fertilizer use). Main means for GHG savings: reduced energy demand, increased wind share, replacing fossil fuels with residual domestic biomass.
  • Main environmental challenge: supply of biofuels for heavy terrestrial transport, ships, defense and aviation. Energy crops → impacts in global warming (mainly LUC), aquatic eutrophication, land occupation. Fossil diesel preferable to biomass fuels except global warming where FT-biodiesel performed slightly better.
  • LCA gives clear objective understanding of impacts of choices; strong tool to compare policy alternatives; widely usable in transport policy & planning.
Important Distinctions to Remember (L35)
  • CSV (conservative, fossil) vs RME (rapeseed biodiesel) vs BtL (biomass-to-liquid, Fischer–Tropsch).
  • Fossil share 84% / renewable 16% / oil 39% (2008). Transport 34% > households 29% > industry 18%.
  • Fossil diesel preferable to biofuels in most categories, except global warming (FT-biodiesel slightly better).
