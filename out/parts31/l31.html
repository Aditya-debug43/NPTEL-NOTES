L31 — Sustainable Transportation Planning & Approaches-II: Contemporary Planning Process

Planning background

  • s3 Common flaws in planning based only on forecasting & facility expansion (graph labels): A early-stage forecasting (under-estimation); B late-stage forecasting (over-estimation); C "Return to normal" bias. (Graph also shows "forecasting reference year", "forecasting gap", "conventional growth cycle".)
  • s4 Main challenges (UNESCAP 2015): non-inclusive development; difficult to meet growing demand (economic & population growth, rising income – faster than growth of economies); air pollution & GHG emissions growing faster than GDP; adverse health & social impacts; road safety.
  • s5 U.K. Transport Planning Society view – planning should: prepare, assess, implement policies, plans, projects; improve & manage transport systems efficiently; plan at local, regional, national level; understand interrelationship between transport infrastructure and future shape of cities/towns; change people's perspectives/attitude and encourage alternative modes rather than private cars.
  • s6 Sustainable transportation: capacity to support the mobility needs of a society in a manner that is the least damageable to the environment and does not impair the mobility needs of future generations.
  • s7 Sustainable Development → Sustainable Transportation (linked with sustainable transport Modes, Infrastructures, Operations). Dimensions: Environment (climate change, air quality, noise, land use, waste); Economy (materials & energy, growth, employment, pricing, competitiveness); Society (safety, health, disturbance, access, equity).

The contemporary transport planning process

  • s8 Commonly scale-specific and multi-dimensional. Traffic problems increased significantly since the 1970s despite much urban planning. Early planning was based on induced demand (to enhance capacities) rather than better management/maintenance of existing systems. Preference: Accessibility rather than Mobility.
  • s9–10 Multi-stage process (5 stages): 1) Goals & Objectives – accessibility with improving safety & health, reducing vehicular emissions, enhancing economic opportunities, improving livability, promoting mobility by improving equity. 2) Set of possible options – what objectives are desirable & what they imply; several alternate scenarios considered. 3) Identification of actors, institutions & stakeholders – those affected, possible resolutions, public participation. 4) Predicting outcomes, identifying benefits, assessing costs – critical step; e.g. visual impacts, environmental dislocations, employment impacts. 5) Choosing a course of action – evaluating scenarios (costs & benefits); political decisions.
  • s11 Urban transport development paths: development takes place within a path dependency → different levels of mobility & ownership (cars, buses, rail transit); once a path is elected, future development is locked in.
  • s12 Benefits of reducing car dependency: healthier & active lifestyle; reduces demand for car parking; improves air & noise quality; improves safety on roads; reduction in traffic levels; provides improved alternative choices for travel; improves accessibility to destinations by all modes.
  • s13–14 Planning: dichotomy → continuum. Megalopolises (mega-urban regions) = massive urban conurbations often encompassing several EMRs, structured along a corridor. Ex.: BostWash (Boston–Washington), Tokaido (Tokyo–Osaka), Pearl River Delta (Hong Kong–Guangzhou), Shanghai–Nanjing. Spatial imprint of transport varies with socio-economic & geographical characteristics; spatial interaction depends on circulation pattern of passengers & freight. Ex.: North American cities – urban form shaped by the automobile, dominance of highways.
  • s15 City connectivity from the CBD (CBD = Central Business District); map legend groups: Production & Distribution (port, heavy industry, railyards, manufacturing, distribution clusters); Mobility & Accessibility (transit stations, shopping, airport districts); Transactions (financial/management districts).
  • s16 Cities may be Polycentric (more common) or Monocentric. Traffic flows may be Organized or disorganized (more common).
  • s17 Street networks: Grid pattern was relatively not common in 19th & early 20th century. Grid: reduction in level of connectivity & level of landuse [as stated]. Cul-de-sac planning reduces/eliminates through movements; cul-de-sacs = dead-end streets reducing extra traffic movement mainly in residential areas.
  • s18 One-hour commuting by different modes: a theoretical urban form = summation of successive transport influences; assumes uniform friction of distance and capacity of transport infrastructure.

Achieving sustainable urban transport planning s21–23

  • Framework diagram (Gudmundsson 2014): Governance (political, institutional, regulatory) → System (infrastructure, energy, vehicles) and Demand (land use, economy, life style) → Activity (distance, mode, speed) → Impacts (economic, social, environmental).
  • Governance of transport infrastructure is critical because of its strategic, economic & social importance. Interrelationship between transport modes and infrastructures should be considered in planning.

Transport Demand Management (TDM) s24–25

  • Manage both demand and the transport system, not only demand as in earlier planning. High population densities favour walking, cycling, public transport. Densification & integration: planning concentrated along well-served transport corridors (TOD – transit-oriented development) and increasing densities in areas under rehabilitation.
  • TDM is done through (1) traffic calming measures and (2) traffic management strategies (continued in L32).

Traffic calming measures s26–61

  • Main idea: reduce speed of flowing traffic or reduce cut-through traffic by obstructing movements in one or more directions. Four general types: Vertical deflections, Horizontal shifts, Narrowing roadways (these three intended to reduce speed) and Closures (intended to reduce cut-through traffic).
MeasureKey facts from slides
Speed hump s27Vertical obstruction; rounded & raised pavement; also "road humps/undulations"; slows cars to about 30 km/h or lower nearby; not typically used on major roads, bus routes, primary emergency response routes. (Fig. s29: hump = gradual raised area, low-speed roads, residential/local streets; 3–4 inches high, 10–12 feet; used on residential streets with speed limits up to 25 mph.)
Speed bump s28Similar to hump but more aggressive; forces vehicles to slow much more than humps; used in parking lots, residential streets etc. (Fig. s29: abrupt raised area, private driveways/parking lots, private residential developments & shopping centers; 4–6 inches high, 1–3 feet.)
Speed table s30Like humps but long, raised, flat section in middle, ramps on ends. At pedestrian crossing = raised crosswalk; only one direction of road = offset speed table.
Curb extension / bulb-out s31–33Horizontal extension of sidewalk into street → narrower roadway; easier/safer pedestrian crossing (shortens curb-to-curb distance); at mid-block = choker. Gives space for benches & street lighting.
Diagonal diverter s34–36Barriers placed diagonally across four-legged intersections, blocking through movements; also full diverters / diagonal road closures; can be partial closure allowing only two-wheelers and obstructing four-wheelers.
Lateral shift s37–38Realignment of straight street so travel lanes shift in ≥1 direction. Suitable: local, collector, arterial roads; one-lane one-way & two-lane two-way; with/without bicycle facilities; max appropriate speed limit typically 35 mph; along bus transit routes.
Chicane s39–40Series of alternating curves/lane shifts forcing motorist to steer back and forth (extra turns). Mid-block (entire block if short); speed limit typically 35 mph or less; one-lane one-way & two-lane two-way; open or closed (curb & gutter) cross-section.
Road closures s41–43Full closure: barrier across street closing it to through traffic, usually leaving space for pedestrians/bicyclists; also called cul-de-sacs, dead-ends, mini-parks. Half closure: barrier blocks travel in one direction (creates one-way street) for a short distance on two-way street; also partial closures / one-way closures.
Median barrier / forced turn island s44–46Raised islands along street centreline continuing through an intersection; blocks through movement from cross street; also called median diverter, intersection barrier, intersection diverter, island diverter. (Examples: with pedestrian refuge & bicycle cut-through.)
Median island s47–48Raised island along centreline that narrows travel lanes at that location; same alternate names; separates opposing traffic and slows by briefly narrowing roadway; may have crosswalk.
Mini roundabout s49–50Raised islands in unsignalized intersections around which traffic circulates; drivers slow to comfortably maneuver.
Roundabout s51–52Raised islands large in diameter, unsignalized intersections; can serve high volumes of large trucks & buses depending on design; different from traffic circles or mini-roundabouts; possible substitute for traffic signal control; encourages steady flow; may have statues, fountains, community gardens.
On-street parking s53–55Allocation of paved space to parking; narrows travel lanes, increases side friction; one or both sides; parallel or angled, but parallel generally preferred for maximized speed reduction.
Raised pedestrian crossing / intersection s56–57Flat raised areas covering entire intersections, ramps on all approaches, often brick/textured; raised to sidewalk height → slows vehicles, easier crossing; also called raised junctions, intersection humps, plateaus; same-level crossing for all pedestrians & specially abled.
Road diet s58–61Revision of lane use/widths → one travel lane per direction with minimum practical width, goal: reduce cross-section. Ex.: four-lane two-way road → three-lane road (two through lanes + centre two-way left-turn lane, TWLTL). May narrow lanes; alternate uses: bicycle facilities, left-turn lanes, on-street parking, raised medians, pedestrian refuge islands, sidewalks. Seattle Rainier Avenue: 4-mile dangerous lane, drivers at 40 mph+; after modification speeds decreased by approx. 95% [as stated].

Conclusion s62

  • Contemporary planning ensures sustainable development by emphasising accessibility rather than mobility; focus on management of existing traffic & infrastructure rather than mere capacity enhancement; traffic calming ensures safe planning by controlling traffic speed and volume; traffic management strategies should be combined with calming and policy measures.
Important Distinctions to Remember (L31)
  • Hump vs bump: hump = gradual, residential/local, ~30 km/h; bump = abrupt, more aggressive, parking lots/driveways.
  • Speed table = flat top + ramps; raised crosswalk (at pedestrian crossing) vs offset speed table (one direction only).
  • Curb extension at mid-block = choker. Full closure (cul-de-sac/mini-park) vs half closure (one-way).
  • Lateral shift (single realignment, ≤35 mph) vs chicane (series of alternating curves).
  • Mini roundabout vs roundabout (large diameter; substitute for signals). Calming types: 3 reduce speed, closures reduce cut-through traffic.
