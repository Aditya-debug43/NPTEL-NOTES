Lecture 11 — Introduction to Environment Impact Assessment (EIA) (47 slides)

Development vs environment; EIA as middle path L11·s3–8

  • Choice posed (s3): Development (poverty reduction, employment, ease of living) vs Environment (environment protection, biodiversity, human health). "Unplanned developments negatively affect the environment" (s4).
  • Aarey Forest (s5): approx. 800-acre land in Mumbai; 2600 trees to be cut for metro shed; protest erupted after 1500 trees were cut; government later changed decision and declared it reserved forest land.
  • Kudankulam Nuclear Power Plant (s6): project to reduce dependence on coal-based thermal plants; villagers protested citing negative environmental impact; example of regional/national interest vs local interests.
  • Madhyam-Pratipada (s7): Buddha — "enlightenment by avoiding the extremes of self-gratification on one hand and self-mortification on the other" = middle path; answer to development-vs-environment = balancing the two sides. EIA = balancing process between developmental and environmental goals (s8).

Definition, need, purpose L11·s9–14

  • EIA: a tool for decision making to find the best possible alternative in terms of environmental as well as economic costs and benefits.
  • Definition (Barry Sadler 1996): "a process of identifying, predicting, evaluating, and mitigating the biophysical, social, and other relevant effects of proposed projects and physical activities prior to major decisions and commitments being made."
  • Need (s10): EIA aims to minimize environmental and social impacts and can enhance sustainability development where large-scale projects are implemented (IISD 2016). s11: impacts of transportation projects — Economic = generally positive; Social = positive/negative; Environmental = generally negative.
  • Purpose (s12): (1) facilitate decision-making – systematic examination of environmental implications of a proposed action and alternatives before a decision; (2) aid in formation of sustainable development – framework for considering location, design and environmental issues in parallel; aid to formulation of developmental actions; environmentally sensitive developments; (3) instrument for sustainable development – overall quality of life; continuing access to natural resources; avoiding lasting environmental damage.
  • Core values (s13): Integrity (fair, unbiased, balanced); Utility (balanced, credible information for decision making); Sustainability (should result in protecting the environment).
  • Components (s14): 1 Resource capability and ecosystem mapping; 2 Natural hazard and landscape sensitivity evaluation; 3 EIA and other forms of impact assessment; 4 Ecosystem approaches (e.g. critical zone, habitat supply).

Lecture 12 — EIA Processes-I (51 slides)

Stages L12·s3–6

  • Stages of EIA (s3): Project Proposal → Screening → Scoping → Baseline Data → Impact Assessment (this lecture) → Mitigation → Impact Statement → Review → Follow-up (next lecture).
  • Flow chart (s4, Kalita 2016): Proposal identification → Screening → (EIA required / initial environmental examination / No EIA) → Scoping → Impact analysis → Mitigation and impact management → EIA report → Review → Decision-making → Approved (Implementation and follow up) or Not approved (Redesign → Resubmit). Public involvement typically at scoping and review (may occur at any stage). Information from follow-up contributes to effective future EIA.
  • EIA phases (s5): Screening (does project require EIA?) → Baseline studies (establish environmental baseline) → Alternatives (consider different approaches) → Impact prediction (forecast impacts) → Impact assessment (interpreting impacts) → Mitigation (what can be done to alleviate negative impacts) → EIS preparation/review (document EIA findings) → Public consultation (general public and NGOs) → Monitoring.
  • Project life cycle (s6): Phase A Pre-feasibility (site selection, environmental screening, initial assessment, scoping of significant issues) → Phase B Feasibility (detailed assessment of significant impacts, identification of mitigation needs, input to cost/benefit analysis) → Phase C Design and engineering (detailed design of mitigation measures) → Phase D Implementation (implementation of mitigation measures and environmental management strategy) → Phase E Monitoring and evaluation (monitoring, post-auditing, lessons for future projects) → back to project concept.

Project proposal; clearance L12·s7–10

  • Define project and related activities; specify zone of impact (direct and indirect); understand activities closely related to project.
  • Environmental clearance process (s8): Start → Site selection → Conduct EIA → Apply for NOC → SPCB arranges public hearing → Project proponent applies for environmental clearance with EIA report, NOC from SPCB, etc. → Review by Environmental Appraisal Committee → Accepted / Rejected / Change suggested (loop back to conduct EIA).
  • Zones (s9–10): Net project area ⊂ Total project area ⊂ Area of direct influence. Highway: net project area = right of way; total project area = area up to buildings; area of direct influence = area affected by noise, air pollution or otherwise.

Screening L12·s11–27

  • Decide whether to conduct the assessment (likely significant impacts) or not (anticipated absence of such impacts); must follow procedures often described in legislation (local laws).
  • Flowchart (s12): screening identifies environmental issues; determines whether EIA needed; establishes need for project → Not require EIA / Require EIA / Impacts unclear. Threshold criteria: size, location, output, cost/finances, environmental effects etc. Impact criteria: significant but easily identifiable impacts; significant impacts; sensitive area. (Project → environmental, economic, social impact.)
  • s13: oil extraction, mining or bullet-train project will have significant impacts; s14: Mussoorie tourist project example (increased vehicular traffic, air traffic, visitors/hospitality, commercial traffic). Screening is done in a predetermined manner to ease the complex process.
  • Parameters (s15): type of project (mining, river training, transport etc.), size of project (cost, length of road, project area), potential impacts (long/short term, moderate/severe, reversible/irreversible).
  • Standardized approach (s16–17): 1 activities outlined in legislation and regulations; 2 legal/policy definitions of proposals; 3 inclusion lists (with or without thresholds); 4 exclusion lists (insignificant or exempt by law e.g. national security/emergency). Examples: all nuclear-substance activities → compulsory EIA (e.g. nuclear power plant); projects needing resettlement (e.g. reservoir); four-or-more-lane highway > 100 km; border infrastructure exempted in India.
  • Customized approach (s18–21): case-by-case with indicative guidance; categories (A, B, C or 1, 2, 3). High impact: significant adverse impacts (irreversible damage, vulnerable ethnic minorities, involuntary resettlement, cultural heritage sites) e.g. large industrial plants, reclamation/resettlement/new land development, thermal and hydropower, pesticides/hazardous materials. Medium impact: limited EIA to identify mitigation – e.g. rural electrification transmission, small-scale aquaculture/irrigation/drainage, renewable energy, tourism, rural water supply/sanitation. Low impact: minimal/no impacts; no EIA required – e.g. small buildings, community garden, community wells, outdoor recreation.

Scoping L12·s28–32

  • Scoping: systematic exercise that establishes the boundaries of the EIA; sets basis of analyses at each stage; sets baseline and identifies alternatives; reduces risk of including inappropriate or excluding needed components. Important outcomes: area of study; factors for assessment for baseline studies.
  • Example: Char-Dham All Weather Road (Uttarakhand) – significant regional environmental, economic, social impacts → very large scope boundary.
  • Scoping flowchart (s30) → TOR report: Project description · Project site (location on map) · Project alternatives (sites, infrastructure, technologies) · Determination of potential impacts · Analysis and evaluation of risks · Formulation of Environmental Management Plan · Mitigation plan · Monitoring plan · Resettlement Action Plan (if relevant) → TOR (Terms of Reference) report.
  • TOR (s31) = roadmap for EIA preparation, covering issues identified in scoping: project description; agencies/ministries overseeing EIA and deciding; geographic area (impact zone); EIA requirements in laws; impacts/issues to study; mitigation and/or monitoring systems; provisions for public involvement; key stakeholders; timeframe; expected work product/deliverables; budget.
  • Methods of scoping (s32, Bai & Bai 2014): making a plan for public involvement → assembling relevant information → distribute information to affected interest → evaluating significance of issues → developing a strategy for addressing issues → establishing priorities for environmental assessment → identifying the major issues.

Baseline; impact prediction/assessment L12·s33–48

  • Baseline study: collect all relevant information on current status of the environment; sound basis for design/evaluation of post-EIA studies (e.g. early-stage monitoring); decide a timeline for predictions (e.g. number of years of project operations).
  • Impact prediction: forecasting likely changes in the environment; comparing baseline data with alternatives is done under impact prediction; impacts change over time – assessed over lifetime: construction → operation → even after closure (e.g. nuclear power plants).
  • Impact assessment (s35): based on key environmental, social, cultural characteristics; itemized list of impacts incl. cumulative effects, social impacts, health risks; direct, indirect and cumulative; leads to detailed list of mitigation actions. Example: Ahmedabad–Mumbai bullet train.
  • Environmental factors (s37–40): Land use and soil (soil removal and pollution; land-cover change: wetlands, floodplains, mangroves, subsoil; surface; topography – altitude, gradients, relief, orientation); example Sundarbans mangroves. Water (sewage release treated/untreated; groundwater – aquifers, recharge areas, level of use; surface water – areas affected, use, drainage; floods, sedimentation, erosion, eutrophication). Air (emissions: noise, odor, dust; climate factors: rainfall, temperature, radiation, fog, wind; sources of pollutants; fragile areas). Biodiversity (endangered species; trees and canopy, migratory birds, indicator species, invasive species).
  • Socio-cultural (s41): e.g. tribes of Andaman & Nicobar preserved by not allowing external interventions. Socio-economic (s42): present land use and zoning; land-use plans (master plans); future trends/development pressures; resettlements (Sardar Sarovar dam – mass resettlement, agitations). Historic/heritage (s43–44): community impacts (poor, low-income); impacts on culture/heritage; sites of archaeological value; visual resources; natural areas of scenic value; listed historic areas; other impacts (access to jobs, food, energy, aesthetics); examples Hazrat Ganj (Lucknow), Taj Mahal (Agra).
  • World Bank impact table for a town project (s46): Social environment – traffic, development of social economy; land use; demolition and resettlement, traffic, irrigation works. Living environment – traffic, exchange, employment, income and safety. Ecological – water and soil loss. Water – water pollution (construction/operation), accident risk. Acoustic – traffic noise. Air quality – raised dust, TSP; harmful substances in tail gas (NOx, CO).
  • Approaches (s47–48): World Bank – findings of country-specific environmental studies; national environmental/climate action plans, policy framework, legislation, institutional capabilities; obligations under international treaties. India: NAPCC missions (National Solar Mission, National Water Mission, Green India Mission). EU – compare baseline and alternatives; assess impacts qualitatively and quantitatively; group alternatives with same impacts and highlight differences. EU = political & economic union of 27 European countries with single currency.
  • Conclusion (s49): EIA = multi-stage process identifying externalities, especially negative impacts, suggesting mitigation; screening, scoping, baseline collect data to predict impacts.

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Screening (is EIA needed?) vs Scoping (boundaries; TOR) vs Baseline (current status) vs Prediction (forecast).
  • Standardized (inclusion/exclusion lists) vs Customized (case-by-case categories A/B/C).
  • World Bank A (significant; type/location/sensitivity/scale) / B (potential adverse on populations or important areas) / C (minimal; no further EA).
  • Net project area (right of way) < total project area (up to buildings) < area of direct influence.

Lecture 13 — EIA Processes-II (43 slides)

Mitigation L13·s3–16

  • Stages: previous lecture = project proposal…impact assessment; this lecture = Mitigation, Impact Statement, Review, Follow-up.
  • Mitigation process: impact assessment reveals possible damaging effects; mitigation = measures to reduce or remove adverse environmental impacts. Example: roadside plantations on highways reduce noise, limit dust, increase green cover — National Green Highway Mission.
  • Mitigation measures are: (1) preventive measures that avoid impacts; (2) measures limiting severity and duration; (3) compensation mechanisms for unavoidable impacts. Example: Project Tiger (Govt. of India).
  • Objectives (s6): identify actions that most reduce impacts and are practical/feasible; reduce severity to lowest possible level over project lifetime and avoid irreversible and cumulative effects; include activities to monitor compliance; identify risks of accidents, malfunctions, emergencies in all phases.
  • Project Tiger (s7–8): rapid decline of tigers due to urbanization/development; launched 1973; positive results later. Tiger counts 2010→2014: Sunderbans 70→76; Northern Bengal —→3; Uttarakhand 227→340; Uttar Pradesh 118→117; Bihar 8→28; Andhra Pradesh 72→68; Chhattisgarh 26→46; Madhya Pradesh 257→308; Maharashtra 169→190; Odisha 32→28; Rajasthan 36→45; Jharkhand 10→3; Karnataka 300→406; Kerala 71→136; Tamil Nadu 163→229; Goa —→5; Assam 143→167; Arunachal Pradesh —→28; Mizoram 5→3.
  • Mitigation hierarchy (s9, BBOP 2009): predicted impacts → Step 1 Avoid → Step 2 Mitigate → Step 3 Restore → Step 4 Offset → No net loss; further Contribution = Net gain.
  • Examples: National Green Highway Mission (greening of NH corridors with farmers, private sector, Forest dept.) (s10); replanting vegetation on slopes; trees to limit noise/dust around housing (s11); noise barriers along rail lines – especially if rail passes through populated area; Delhi metro has used it (s12); wildlife passages – help animals navigate, maintain natural drainage, help local population access nearby locations (s13); bamboo/rope bridge for reptiles (snakes, lizards) in Uttarakhand (s14); smog towers (Delhi) to reduce pollutants in ambient air (s15); flora conservation – Western Ghats most diverse tropical region (s16).

Impact management; R&R; contingency L13·s17–24

  • Impact Management Plan (IMP): series of plans and protocols to manage and monitor identified mitigation measures and risks. Implementation depends on: type of project; identified impacts; risks associated; natural and social context.
  • Elements (s19): Mitigation measures (summary, linked to impact analyses) → Monitoring (plans for monitoring impacts and effectiveness of mitigation) → Capacity development (capacity-building needs to involve stakeholders) → Implementation schedule and cost estimates → Integration (EMP fits with planned project and mitigation actions).
  • Resettlement & Rehabilitation (R&R) (s20–21): minimize number of people affected/displaced; collaborate with local authorities; community outreach; prepare for new jobs if livelihood changes; prepare for living in new region; overall similar or better lifestyle.
  • Contingency plan (s22): clearly establishes lines of action, responsibilities, functions and quick effective responses to emergencies; includes assessment of possible risks and external contingencies (natural events, disasters) and potential threats to population health and ecosystem structure. Examples: industrial accidents/fires protocols; flood, earthquake safety.
  • Bhopal Gas Tragedy (s24): 1984; highly toxic Methyl Isocyanate (MIC) gas leaked from pesticide plant (Union Carbide); 3787 died, 574336 severely affected; proper contingency plan could have saved many lives.

EIS, review, public participation L13·s25–35

  • EIS (defined by International Chamber of Commerce as a management tool of systematic, documented, periodic and objective evaluation…) — final stage of an EIA. Ideal contents: executive summary; policy, legal and administrative framework; description of environment; detailed description of project; significant environmental impacts; identification and analysis of alternatives; mitigation action/management plan; environmental management plan; monitoring program; knowledge gaps; public involvement; list of references; appendices (reference documents, photographs, unpublished data); Terms of Reference; consulting team composition; notes of public consultation sessions.
  • EIS report formats shown for IDB (Inter-American Development Bank), EU, World Bank (s26–28): image text not preserved (blank in PDF).
  • EIS review (s29): by competent authority as per legal requirements; in India by the government (MoEF). s30 "Objectives of EIS Review" lists 1–4 but item text is absent from PDF.
  • Public participation (s31–34): goal = engage key stakeholders (citizens, NGOs, agencies, authorities, interest groups) to input on planned development, especially impacts affecting livelihoods. In practice consultation is done mainly during review stage (s32). Features of effective participation (s33, Aschemann 2004): inform and involve interested/affected stakeholders; explicitly address comments, concerns and inputs (in documentation and decision making); ensure all relevant actors/parties involved or represented; safeguard sufficient access to all relevant documents and information. s34 steps (World Bank 2010): image text absent.
  • Licensing (s35): the outcome of the review process.

Follow-up / monitoring L13·s36–40

  • Follow-up/Monitoring ensures: accuracy of original predictions; degree of deviation; possible reasons for deviation; whether mitigation achieved its objectives. Information contributes to improvement of future EIA (more accurate predictions).
  • Monitoring process shown for World Bank (s37, text absent), Asian Development Bank (international organization which funds development projects across Asian countries, s38), EBRD – European Bank for Reconstruction & Development (s39).
  • General shortcomings of EIA reports (s40, Netherlands EIA Commission): description of proposal does not cover key features (e.g. omits pipeline/facilities); key problems affected by the proposal not described; sensitive elements in affected environment overlooked; alternatives do not comply with environmental regulations/standards; appropriate mitigating measures not considered; serious environmental impacts or risks not described or incorrectly described; insufficient or outdated prediction models used.
  • Conclusion (s41): EIA balances development and environment; without EIA harm to environment and indigenous population; proper implementation ensures sustainable growth and community benefits.

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Mitigation hierarchy order: Avoid → Mitigate → Restore → Offset (no net loss) → Contribution (net gain).
  • IMP (manage/monitor mitigation) vs contingency plan (emergencies) vs R&R (displaced people).
  • EIS = final stage of EIA; review by competent authority (MoEF in India); licensing = outcome of review; public consultation mainly at review.
  • Project Tiger 1973; Bhopal 1984 (3787 died; 574336 affected).

Lecture 14 — Methodologies of EIA (45 slides)

List of methods L14·s2–3

8 methods: Ad hoc · Checklist · Matrix · Network · Overlay · Environmental index (factor analysis) · Cost/Benefit analysis · Simulation.

Ad-hoc method L14·s4–10

  • Approach (s4): indicate broad areas of possible impacts → list composite environmental parameters likely to be affected → team of specialists who identify impacts in their area of expertise → nature of impacts considered.
  • Features: intuitive and qualitative; generally used in screening process.
  • Common criteria (s6): air quality, noise, health and safety, groundwater, natural drainage, endangered species, wildlife, natural vegetation, economic values, exotic vegetation, social characteristics, recreation, visual description, grazing, public facilities, open space, services.
  • Common practice: committee of experts decides by consensus or majority. USA: team of specialists with minimal guidance beyond NEPA requirements. India: similar process during screening to decide whether to do impact assessment.
  • Alternative reservoir example (s9): 4 alternatives × parameters (air quality, noise, wildlife, local population, water quality); rank 1/2/3/4 each alternative on every parameter and assess best option.
  • Pros/cons (s10): Pro – simple, can be performed without training. Cons – no relative weighting or cause-effect relationship; suggests only broad areas; no assurance all relevant impacts studied; lacks consistency; inefficient (effort to assemble a panel each time).

Overlay method L14·s29–32

  • Set of maps of project area's environmental characteristics (physical, social, ecological, aesthetic); separate mapping of critical features at the same scale as project site plan (wetlands, steep slopes, soils, floodplains, bedrock outcrops, wildlife habitats, vegetative communities, cultural resources).
  • Old technique: features mapped on transparent plastic in different colors. Modern: GIS with skilled personnel; aspects as layers with color coding (Zhang et al. 2011). (Pros/cons s30 absent.)

Simulation method L14·s33–34

  • Micro method for a very specific situation; experimental; scope limited to a single activity; assesses impact of any product or small-scale service. Example: electric vs fossil-fuel vehicles compared in standard conditions for exact emissions and cost per km.

Cost-benefit analysis L14·s35–38

  • Social Cost-Benefit Analysis (SCBA): assessing desirability of projects in public as opposed to private sector; identification of costs and benefits (social, environmental, economic); measurement in monetary terms; effect of risk and uncertainty during project life cycle; generally used by international institutions such as World Bank.
  • Stages of SCBA (s37): 1 financial profitability based on market prices → 2 shadow prices for resources to arrive at net benefit at economic process → 3 adjustment of net benefit for projects impact → 4 adjustment for impact on income distribution → 5 adjustment for goods whose social values differ from economic values.
  • Limitations (s38): measurement of social costs/benefits formidable; intangible, monetary evaluation subjective; comparison with future projects difficult; impacts differ from case to case.

Environment index method; EPI L14·s39–42

  • Standards predefined; ranking for various parameters against set standards; alternatives ranked by overall score. Based on this, EPI (Environment Performance Index) ranks countries.
  • EPI: international ranking mechanism assessing countries' efforts against climate change; uses distance-to-target technique (best and worst performance = scores 100 and 0); 32 indicators into 11 issue categories and two policy objectives, with weights as % of total score.
  • Weights (s41): Environmental Health 40% (Air quality 20%; Sanitation & drinking water 16%; Heavy metals 2%; Waste management 2%); Ecosystem Vitality 60% (Climate change 24%; Biodiversity & habitat 15%; Ecosystem services 6%; Fisheries 6%; Water resources 3%; Agriculture 3%; Pollution emissions 3%). Indicators shown include PM2.5 exposure 11%, household solid fuels 8%, sanitation 6.4%, drinking water 9.6%, CO2 13.2%, CH4 3.6%.
  • EPI ranking map (s42) bands: 1–36, 37–72, 73–108, 109–144, 145–180, NA.
  • Conclusion (s43): methodologies chosen by project requirements/conditions, time constraints, data and resources; each has benefits and limitations.

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Ad hoc (intuitive, qualitative, expert team; screening) vs Checklist (screening & scoping; simple/descriptive/scaling) vs Matrix (components × actions) vs Network (impact chains; Grand Network Index) vs Overlay (maps; GIS) vs Simulation (micro, single activity) vs CBA (monetary) vs Index/EPI (predefined standards, ranks).
  • Simple (no guidelines) vs Descriptive (measurement/prediction info) vs Scaling (weights).
  • EPI: 32 indicators, 11 categories, 2 objectives; Environmental Health 40% / Ecosystem Vitality 60%.

Lecture 16 — Global practices in EIA process (40 slides)

SEA, screening, cumulative impacts L16·s3–16

  • SEA legal provisions (s3): integrated within EIA legislation – Kenya, Tanzania; separate laws – Indonesia, Canada; integrated in other sectoral legislations (transport, energy) – New Zealand.
  • Parameter selection approaches (s4): Environment-centered (listing factors/criteria for likely impact); Development-centered (listing development activities); Hybrid (between the two). India follows hybrid approach for screening (checklist has both) (s5–6): examples – emissions from fossil fuel combustion, production processes, materials handling, construction, dust/odours (environment-centred); land-use change, clearance of land/vegetation/buildings, new land uses, pre-construction investigations, construction works (development-centred). EU checklist (s7) is also hybrid (physical changes, natural resource use, harmful substances, solid waste, release of pollutants).
  • EU (s8): member countries classify projects into two categories (I & II); case-by-case examination, threshold criteria, or hybrid.
  • Peru (s9): three categories – I: only EIS (low impacts); II: Semi-detailed EIA (EIA-SD; moderate impacts); III: Detailed EIA (EIA-D; significant impacts) (Law 27446, Art. 4).
  • Kenya (s10): one of the few requiring an environmental practitioner at screening stage; knowingly giving false/misleading information is an offence (law text: imprisonment up to 24 months or fine not more than two million shillings or both).
  • Cumulative impacts at scoping (s11–12): regional perspective rather than local. China – no mention of cumulative impact assessment; Canada – study of cumulative effects of physical activities in a region (Canadian EIA legislation 2012). Example: Delhi winter pollution rise partly from stubble burning in neighboring states → cumulative impacts need regional-level management.
  • Mitigation for individual impacts (s13–14): China – mitigation considered during scoping; pollution-control installations must be designed, built, commissioned (Environment Protection Act 2014, Arts 40-42). Examples: noise barriers; treating sludge before dumping in rivers; efficient machines.
  • Offsets (s15–16): Brazil – projects subject to licensing must offset impacts on protected areas. Mexico – Sustainable Forest Development Act 2003 requires offsets for land-use change in forests; Environmental Liability Act 2013 requires offsets only when impacts not predicted/approved in EIA and are an environmental offence. Example Antamina mines (Peru): Polylepis shrub habitat indirectly impacted; EIA: overall impacts limited, key concern = destruction of endangered Polylepis; large-scale conservation programme carried out.
  • Responsibility for EIA reports (s17): government agency – USA; licensed/registered consultants – Vanuatu, Cameroon, Peru, China, Mongolia, Oman, Lebanon; individual with certification – Indonesia. India: agencies registered under QCI or notified by MoEF&CC.

Public participation; review; follow-up; cultural impacts L16·s18–27

  • Canada (s18): public invited to comment for 20 days after project subject to screening and at other stages; comments must be considered by Canadian Environmental Assessment Agency; multi-stage, from screening to monitoring (against general practice elsewhere).
  • Definition of public: China (2014 Act) – full EIA text public; hearings/submissions restricted to residents, environmental groups and others at the project site likely affected. Denmark – natural/legal persons directly or indirectly affected; associations/organizations with protection of landscape, cultural heritage, natural/environmental interests as objective.
  • Baltimore light rail (s21): 52 pre-scoping "Listening Tours"; initial scoping meetings/written comments; published reports; community liaisons; four public hearings; Citizens' Advisory Council; Speaker's Bureau; resource hubs.
  • EIA review (s22–23): EU guideline – review EIS before decision, by govt agencies or professionals on behalf of govt. Cameroon – ESIA report to Ministry of Environment, Nature Protection and Sustainable Development; on-site visits to verify; outcomes to Inter-ministerial Committee.
  • UNEP best features for follow-up/monitoring (s24): sufficient information at regular intervals; manage adverse impacts using monitoring data; ensure indigenous community participation; expand powers of competent enforcement agencies; create regional monitoring networks.
  • Cultural impacts: Canada (s25) – for aboriginal peoples: effects on health and socio-economic conditions; physical and cultural heritage; current use of lands/resources for traditional purposes; structure/site/thing of historical, archaeological, paleontological or architectural significance. Laurentia Project (s26) – Port of Quebec deep-water wharf, Beauport: EIA found significant adverse effects (fish and habitat, air quality and human health, socio-economic conditions, traditional use by Indigenous peoples) → project not commissioned. New Zealand (s27) – effects on aboriginal/indigenous tribal peoples: health & socio-economic; physical & cultural heritage; traditional land use; historical/archaeological/paleontological/architectural sites; impact on community knowledge and aboriginal traditional knowledge.

EIA basics (L11–L13)

  • EIA (Sadler 1996): identifying, predicting, evaluating, mitigating biophysical, social and other effects prior to major decisions. Middle path (Madhyam-Pratipada); balances development vs environment.
  • Core values: Integrity, Utility, Sustainability. Purpose: facilitate decision-making; aid sustainable development; instrument for sustainable development.
  • Types: Rapid (1 season) / Comprehensive (4 seasons); Regional, Sectoral, Strategic (SEA = EIA of PPP), Project-level, LCA. EIA end-of-cycle, reactive vs SEA early, proactive.
  • Impact classes: beneficial/detrimental; reversible/irreversible; repairable/irreparable; short/long term; local/regional/national.
  • Stages: Project proposal → Screening → Scoping → Baseline → Impact prediction/assessment → Mitigation → EIS → Review (public consultation) → Licensing → Follow-up/monitoring.
  • Screening: is EIA needed? (threshold & impact criteria; inclusion/exclusion lists; categories A/B/C). Scoping: boundaries → TOR. Baseline: current status. World Bank A/B/C; C e.g. PMGSY village roads.
  • Mitigation: prevent / limit severity & duration / compensate. Hierarchy Avoid → Mitigate → Restore → Offset. IMP elements: mitigation measures, monitoring, capacity development, implementation schedule & cost, integration. EIS = final stage; review by MoEF (India); licensing = outcome of review; follow-up checks accuracy of predictions.
