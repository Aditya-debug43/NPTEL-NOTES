Lecture 8 — Impacts of Transportation Systems-III: Flora & Fauna (51 slides)

Classification of impacts L8·s3

Four main effects on wildlife L8·s5–6 (EEA 2011)

  1. Habitat loss 2. Traffic mortality 3. Population subdivision 4. Barrier effect — reduce population size and increase risk of extinction. After a time lag (order of decades): population size smaller, greater relative fluctuations over time, more vulnerable.

Direct impacts in detail

ImpactKey points
Loss of wildlife habitat (s7–9)Natural habitat significantly altered; may seem negligible at larger scale but serious locally (depends on location of infrastructure, affected habitat & species). Infrastructure on hilly areas has greater consequences. With increasing infrastructure density, undisturbed habitat areas shrink and become inaccessible. Remnant fragments may become too small/isolated → local extinction. Critical threshold of road density is species-specific, also depends on landscape & infrastructure characteristics.
Habitat fragmentation / barrier effect (s10–13)Roads/railways impermeable to animals. High-density traffic roads and high-speed railways prevent free passage; limits ability to search for food, shelter, mating. Physical barriers: completely fenced roads/railways; unsuitable surfaces or crossing objects (e.g. road verges). Road cut → sum of two parts < value of initial whole; fragments into weaker sub-units, more vulnerable to invasions and degradation. Theory (s13): increasing traffic increases road accidents linearly, until noise & vehicle movements repel animals from crossings.
Fauna traffic mortality (s14–16)Most evident and well-known negative impact. Mostly affected: rare species moving long distances (e.g. otter, large carnivores such as tigers); seasonal migratory species such as amphibians (frogs, toads). Concentration of casualties depends on: temperature; precipitation/time of day; ecological factors (breeding dispersal, food supply, seasonal migrations, age & sex); location/landscape, road specifications, traffic density, crop rotation. Examples shown: NH 67 (Gundlupet–Ooty); NH 212 (Mysore–Kozhikode).
Aquatic habitat damage (s17–19)Erosion from poorly constructed/rehabilitated sites → downstream siltation, ruining fish spawning beds. Constricted channel flows at crossings make current too fast for some species. Altered flood cycles, tidal flows, water levels upset trophic dynamics (plankton life cycle → food chain). Behavioural barriers: mostly larger species; avoidance of areas near roads/railways, avoid crossing large open spaces.
Corridor restrictions (s20–22)When a road blocks a wildlife corridor: (1) cessation of corridor use, (2) increased mortality by collisions, (3) delay in migration → weakening/disappearance of an entire generation. On busy roads death rate for amphibian/slow-moving populations can be as high as one in ten. Example: transport routes along Rajaji National Park, Uttarakhand.
Disturbance & pollution (s23–24)Mountain environments more sensitive. Hydrological changes (cuttings, embankments → soil erosion); chemical pollution (oxides, hydrocarbons, particulates, heavy metals from exhausts); de-icing salt (sodium, chloride) in winter; herbicides in summer; gasoline/oil leaks in accidents. Olivia oil spill 2011, Tristan da Cunha: 40,170 GT bulk carrier grounded on Nightingale Island; leaked 1400 t Intermediate Fuel + 70 t Marine diesel; oiled ~3700 endangered Northern Rockhopper penguins.
Traffic noise (s25–27)Rarely immediate physiological effect on humans; long exposure → psychological stress → physiological disorder. Wild animals treat noise as sign of humans → avoid (wild reindeer, elk, caribou, brown bear). Birds very sensitive: minimum density near roads; interferes with vocal communication, territorial behaviour, mating success; verge vegetation, adjacent habitat, relief alter noise. Most vulnerable species: large; long-lived; low reproductive rates; habitat specialists; living in open (wetlands) rather than closed (forest) habitats; rare; populations concentrated in few areas.
Visual impacts (s28–29)Artificial lights / vehicle movement. Lighting can deter deer and provide insect food for bats but disrupt plant growth regulation, breeding/behaviour in birds, bats, nocturnal frogs, moths. White (mercury vapour) lamps attract insects → aerial-hawking bats → more collision mortality. Heavy trucks & high-speed trains: intensive discontinuous noise, vibration, visual disturbance that frightens mammals & birds.
Biogeochemical cycle (s30–31)Nutrient/material flow determines ecosystem structure. Road development disrupts via alteration of surface & groundwater flows, removal of biomass, relocation of topsoil. Human activity can be a nutrient source → may increase turbidity and BOD. Impacts only roughly estimated based on: nature of alteration; data on soil erodibility; soil fertility; anticipated human activity; understanding of nutrient regime & energy flow.

Indirect impacts L8·s32–35 (World Bank 1997)

  • Accessibility: roads increase human–nature contact → ecosystem modification (recreation, forest & mineral exploitation, colonization, urbanization); upgrading existing roads increases people having access.
  • Ecological disequilibrium: importation of new plant/animal species along right-of-way upsets balance; native species face competition; non-native species gain advantage (lack of natural controls) → simplified, more vulnerable ecosystem.
  • Contamination of biota: vehicles contaminate soil, air, water next to road (surface water well beyond); chronic contamination serious for top of food chain due to bioaccumulation.
  • Forest fires: more human activity → more frequent fires; sudden, severe, wide-ranging (e.g. Dehradun, Uttarakhand; fires due to road accidents).

Crop yield L8·s36–37 (Burney & Ramanathan 2013)

  • Rising temperatures and long-lived GHGs → significant negative impact on yields. Short-lived black carbon and ozone affect temperature, precipitation, radiation and can be toxic to plants.
  • Wheat yield in India in 2010 was 36% lower than 1980s; some densely populated states had 50% relative yield losses. 90% of wheat-yield reduction attributed to short-lived climate pollutants. (Relative yield change 2006–2010 average for rice and wheat.)

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Direct (8) vs Indirect (4) impacts. Barrier effect = habitat fragmentation = corridor restriction concept family; four main effects: habitat loss, traffic mortality, population subdivision, barrier effect.
  • Underpass size ↔ animal size: pipe culverts (small) → intermediate (medium: foxes, black bear) → larger (large carnivores) → extensive bridges (grizzly, wolves).
  • Noise-sensitive: birds; avoiders: reindeer, elk, caribou, brown bear. Light: attracts insects/bats (white mercury vapour), deters deer.

Lecture 9 — Impacts of Transportation Systems-IV: Soil, Water, Acid Rain (53 slides)

Soil and water — physical impacts L9·s3–5

  • Construction clears, levels, fills, cuts natural material → changes soil density, landscape relief, surface and groundwater flows, microclimate → alters land cover, vegetation, habitat composition.
  • Wetlands & habitats especially sensitive to hydrology change (embankments, cuttings may drain aquifers, ↑ soil erosion, earth slides); can pollute watercourses with sediments; canalisation of surface water into ditches changes run-off and debris flows.

Chemical impacts L9·s6–8

  • Pollutants: road dust, salt, heavy metals, fertiliser nutrients, toxins. Accumulate near infrastructure but effects on vegetation/fauna seen at distances of several hundreds of metres.
  • De-icing salts (NaCl, CaCl2, KCl, MgCl2): damage vegetation, contaminate drinking water, reduce soil pH → increases mobility of heavy metals.
  • Heavy/trace metals (Pb, Zn, Cu, Cr, Cd, Al from petrol, de-icing salts, dust) accumulate in plant/animal tissues; affect reproduction & survival.
  • Exhaust toxins: PAH, dioxins, ozone, nitrogen, CO2, fertilising chemicals. Emissions in lakes & heathlands at >200 m from road can change plant growth and species diversity.

Water quality — roads L9·s9–11

  • Road drainage & excavation lower the water table; embankments & structures raise it by restricting flow. Can contribute to flooding, soil erosion, channel modification, siltation of streams. Road construction blocks subsurface streams → wetting upflow side and drying downflow side. Improper/inadequate drainage → rising water tables or flooding.

Water transport impacts L9·s12–20

  • Marine transport emissions = most important segment of water-quality impact of transportation sector. Main effects from: dredging, waste, ballast waters, oil spills.
  • Dredging: deepening harbor channels by removing sediments from the bed of a water body. Waste has very high bacteria level – hazardous to public health and marine ecosystems.
  • Vessel emissions: air pollutants; water emissions – cargo hold cleaning water, ballast water, grey water, sewage, oil spills.
  • Ballast water discharge from other parts of the world introduces invasive species → domestic species may go extinct (pollution different from emission type).
  • Oil spills: mostly ship pollution; less frequent than daily-operation pollution but devastating; toxic to marine life. PAHs (components of crude oil) hard to clean, last years in sediment; cause developmental problems, disease susceptibility, abnormal reproductive cycles.

Air transport — water quality L9·s21–22

  • Airports: jet fuel, lubricants, chemicals → water pollution. Cold climates: de-icing fluids fall to ground → storm-water runoff to streams/rivers/coastal waters if not recovered. Active ingredient: ethylene glycol or propylene glycol → high BOD during degradation → consume oxygen needed by aquatic organisms.

Acid rain L9·s23–29

  • Formed when acidic pollutants SO2 and NO2 dissolve in rain water.
  • SO2(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ H2SO3 (sulphurous acid) → with O2 slowly oxidized to H2SO4 (sulphuric acid): H2SO3 + O2 → H2SO4 [as written]. NO2 (with O2, H2O): 4NO2 + 2H2O → 4HNO3 [as written] (nitric acid).
  • Pathway: 1. SO2/NOx emitted → 2. transformed into acid particles, transported long distances → 3. fall as wet and dry deposition (dust, rain, snow) → 4. harm soil, forests, streams, lakes.
Effect onDetail
Land/soilSoil acid levels rise → kills plants.
PlantsStops trees and plants receiving oxygen [as written] → stops growth.
Material assetsDissolves limestone and marble; erodes/destroys sculptures; buildings wither.
Water bodiesLakes acidified easily due to low water flow; most aquatic species cannot survive → biodiversity affected.
HumansOnly indirect effects. SO2 particles → chronic lung problems (asthma, bronchitis); NOx → ground-level ozone → severe lung problems (chronic pneumonia, emphysema).

Mitigation L9·s31–42 (World Bank 2019)

  • Good road drainage features: cutoff/catch-water drain, scour checks, culvert, natural water course, side drain, turn-out drains (s31). Storm water inlets & sewers remove water from road surfaces (s32); highway surface drainage on sides and middle (s33).
  • Infiltration: cross and side drains → groundwater recharge structures (s34); roadside infiltration trenches with bunds intercept surface runoff (s35); tube recharge wells = Bhungroo in Gujarat, India (s36); road water harvesting to surface storage (s38).
  • Irrigation from road drainage (s37): runoff directed to interconnected soaking pits; upper bund breached then lower bund; repeat until all fields watered; smooth water movement to avoid erosion; fields well levelled. Examples: rice drainage via roadside borrow pit – Bangladesh (s39); grapes with road water – Bolivia (s40); collecting road water – Shandong, China (s41); urban lake "Ijzeren Vrouw" from borrow pit – Netherlands (s42).

  • Conclusion (s51): marine & aviation industries can also damage environment like road; low-sulphur and alternate fuels significantly reduce SOx and NOx (acid rain).

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Road drainage lowers water table vs embankments/structures raise it.
  • Propylene/ethylene glycol (airport de-icing; BOD) ≠ road de-icing salts (NaCl etc.; reduce soil pH, mobilise heavy metals).
  • SO2 → H2SO3 → H2SO4; NO2 → HNO3. Humans: only indirect acid-rain effects.
  • Booms (contain) vs skimmers (recover) vs sorbents (absorb) vs dispersants (break into droplets) vs burning vs bioremediation.
  • Haven 1991 (explosion, spark in tank cleaning) vs Gulsar Ana 2009 vs RENA 2011 vs Olivia 2011 (L8).

Lecture 10 — Impact of Transportation Systems-V: Noise (51 slides)

Noise and its effects L10·s3–8

  • Noise = unwanted sound; level of sound exceeding acceptable level and creating annoyance. Main contributors: urbanization and traffic. Noise from vehicles ≈ 70% of total traffic noise. Sources: road & rail traffic, aircraft, industry, construction.
  • Effects in three groups: Subjective, Behavioral, Physiological.
GroupDescription
SubjectiveAnnoyance, disturbance, noisiness; difficult to measure precisely (people perceive differently, e.g. music vs noise).
BehavioralInterference with speech, sleep or other tasks; sleep disturbance in dense traffic areas, near railway tracks or airports.
PhysiologicalSudden shock/frightening effect; headache; extremely high noise for long duration → deafness.
  • Harmful effects on humans (s8): interferes with speech; emotional & behavioral stress; permanent hearing loss; diseases (headache, blood pressure, heart failure); health effects (increased heart beat, constriction of blood vessels, dilation of pupil); problematic for patients needing rest; potential to damage liver, brain and heart.

Factors & sources of vehicular noise L10·s11–20

  • Factors affecting traffic noise are grouped: Traffic factors, Road factors, Vehicle factors, Human factors (s11–16; contents are images). Human factors: irresponsible/inappropriate behavior; 90% of accidents linked to human error (s17).
  • Facts (s13): motor vehicles noisier than passenger cars [as written]; diesel engines noisier than petrol; commercial trucks & loaded vehicles noisier than other road modes; noise directly proportional to age of vehicle (older → more noise).
  • Two major vehicle sources (s18): Power plant & transmission noise (engine, exhaust, intake, cooling system); Running gear noise (tyre–road interaction, differential, propeller shaft). On highways: at lower speeds engine-exhaust noise dominant; at higher speeds road–tyre interaction dominant.

Noise measurement L10·s21–30

  • Sound level meter block chain (s21): microphone → transducer signal → pre-amplifier / filters → weighing network → amplifier → rectifier → averaging system → display (or AC outlet).
  • Acoustic arrays (s22): number of microphones in various patterns; omnidirectional; receive sound from wide area.
  • Decibel (s23): SPL (Lp) = logarithmic measure of effective pressure relative to reference; unit dB. Lp = ln(p/p0) Np = 2 log10(p/p0) B = 20 log10(p/p0) dB; p = rms sound pressure; p0 = reference. 1 B = 10 dB. Reference in air p0 = 20 µPa = threshold of human hearing (≈ mosquito flying 3 m away).
  • Leq (equivalent continuous noise level) (s24–26): formula (s26): Leq = 10 log[ (1/(t2−t1)) ∫ (pA²/p0²) dt ] from t1 to t2 (pA = acquired sound pressure; p0 = 20 µPa). preferred method for time-varying sound; single dB value accounting for total sound energy over time. Example: over 24 h as low as 30 dB(A) at night, 70 dB(A) with occasional vehicles, higher by day. Meter stores fluctuations, calculates "average energy" – not arithmetic average: dB converted to SPL, summed, divided by number of samples, converted back to dB.
  • Energy average example (s27): levels 88, 93 [text] and 97 dBA: arithmetic mean = (88+94+97)/3 = 93 dBA; energy-average Leq = 10 log10{[108.8+109.4+109.7]/3} = 94.3 dBA. [Slide text writes "93" and "94" inconsistently; kept as on slide.]
  • A-weighting dB(A) (s28–29): most common weighting; cuts off lower and higher frequencies average person cannot hear; imitates human ear; ear responds more to 500 Hz–8 kHz, less sensitive to very low/high pitch. Units dBA or dB(A).
  • n-percent exceeded level Ln (s30): sound level exceeded n% of time. L10 > L50 > L90. L10: exceeded 10% of time – sporadic/intermittent events. L50: 50% – median / fluctuating noise. L90: 90% – background/ambient noise.

Mitigating measures L10·s31–33

  • Green plantations (bushes/trees around sources) · Regular servicing & tuning of automobiles · Green buildings (noise-absorbing materials for walls, windows, ceilings) · Wearables (ear plugs, earmuffs) · Lubrication & regular servicing of machinery · Policy regulations (restrict loudspeakers in crowded/public areas) · Soundproof doors/windows · Land use (factories far from residential areas) · Community development programs (long-term planning) · Social awareness programs.

Noise barriers L10·s34–46

  • Barriers reduce sound by blocking straight-line path source→receiver; reduction ≈ 5 dB; further 3 dB by making the barrier much higher (longer path) → combined 8 dB = equivalent to reducing traffic by factor of 6. Diffraction depends on ratio of barrier height h to wavelength λ; higher barrier → more efficient.
  • Heights (s36): min permissible 1–2.5 m; max 4–10 m or more; average (most common) 2–5 m. Shadow zone behind barrier.

  • Transmission loss: sound level reduced when forced to travel through the barrier; 30 dB transmission loss = practically all (99.9%) sound blocked.
  • Insertion loss: difference in sound level without barrier vs after barrier installed between source and receiver.
  • Insertion loss (IL), Kurze & Anderson model (s46): IL = 5 dB + 20 log[ √(2πN) / tanh√(2πN) ] dB for N up to 12.5; IL = 20 dB for N > 12.5. N = Fresnel number = non-dimensional measure of how much farther the sound must travel to reach the receiver due to the barrier; N = (a + b − l)·f / C0; l = original direct path length source→receiver; a, b = the two straight segments of the path modified by the barrier; f = sound frequency (Hz); C0 = speed of sound in air (approx. 1125 ft/sec or 343 m/s).
  • Numerical problem (s47–48): 12 ft barrier, 12 ft from vehicle; house 15 ft beyond barrier; window height 4 ft. l = √(27²+4²) = 27.3 ft; a = √(12²+12²) = 17 ft; b = √(15²+8²) = 17 ft; a+b−l = 34 − 27.3 = 6.7 ft. At f = 100 Hz, N = (6.7 × 100)/1100 = 0.61 [slide uses 1100]; IL = 5 + 20 log[√(2π×0.61)/tanh√(2π×0.61)] ≈ 10 dB.
  • Conclusion (s49): loud, regular exposure → effects from slight disturbance to even death; reduce noise from planning stage; trees/shrubs and barriers help.

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Subjective (annoyance) vs Behavioral (speech/sleep interference) vs Physiological (shock, headache, deafness).
  • Leq = energy average, not arithmetic mean (94.3 vs 93 dBA). L10 (intermittent) > L50 (median) > L90 (background).
  • Transmission loss (through barrier; 30 dB = 99.9% blocked) vs insertion loss (with vs without barrier).
  • Low speeds: engine-exhaust noise dominates; high speeds: tyre–road dominates. Diesel > petrol; older vehicle = noisier.
  • Barrier: 5 dB + 3 dB = 8 dB ≈ traffic ÷ 6. Reference pressure 20 µPa; 1 B = 10 dB.
