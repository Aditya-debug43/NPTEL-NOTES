Lecture 5 — Climate Change: Indicators and Impacts (44 slides)

Basics L5·s3–8

  • Climate change: change in usual weather found in a place (e.g. rain per year, usual temperature for a month/season); also change in Earth's climate (usual temperature; places where rain and snow fall). Weather can change in a few hours; climate takes hundreds or even millions of years to change. (NASA 2014)
  • Causes (s4): Natural (not controllable): Earth's distance from the sun; oceans, volcano eruptions etc. Humans (controllable): anthropogenic activities; GHG emissions.
  • Is warming manmade? (s5): solar energy received followed the Sun's natural 11-year cycle with no net increase since the 1950s, while global temperature rose markedly → "extremely unlikely that the Sun has caused" the warming trend of past half-century.
  • s6: yearly temperature vs twentieth-century average 1880–2019 (red/blue bars) and atmospheric CO2 1880–2019 (grey line). s7: average global temperature & world carbon emissions from fossil fuel burning (millions of tons) 1800–2014.
  • Warmest years (s8): 2020 = 2nd warmest on record; 2016 warmest; 2019 3rd warmest; world's 7 warmest years all since 2014; 10 warmest all since 2005.

Climate change drivers: GHGs L5·s9–13

  • GHGs = most significant driver of observed climate change since the mid-20th century. Human-activity GHG sources: electricity, transportation, agriculture, commercial & residential.
  • Global warming potential (GWP): concept used to convert amounts of other gases into carbon dioxide equivalents.
  • Radiative forcing: the heating effect caused by greenhouse gases in the atmosphere (s12).
  • s11: global atmospheric concentrations of CO2, CH4, N2O & O3 1950–2015 (chart). s13: radiative forcing by human activities (chart).

Climate change indicators L5·s14–26

IndicatorFacts
Weather & climate (s14–15)Rising global average temperature → widespread changes in weather patterns. Extreme events (heat waves, large storms) likely more frequent/intense. Extreme variation = threat (illness to death). Positive: increased precipitation can replenish water supplies and support agriculture. Negative: intense storms damage property, loss of life, displacement, disrupt transportation, telecommunications, energy, water supplies.
Oceans (s16–17)Cover about 70% of Earth's surface. As GHGs absorb more energy, oceans absorb more heat → higher sea surface temperature and rising sea level. Positive: oceans reduce climate change by storing large amounts of CO2. Negative: dissolved carbon makes seawater more acidic; may alter biodiversity and productivity; corals and shellfish find it harder to build skeletons and shells.
Ocean acidity (s18)Ocean CO2 levels rose with atmospheric CO2 → increase in acidity (a decrease in pH).
Ocean heat (s19)Ocean heat content 1955–2015; measured in joules; compared with 1971–2000 average (set to zero).
Sea surface temperature (s20)Increased during 20th century; 1901–2015: rose at average 0.13 °F per decade.
Sea level (s21)Rose throughout 20th century, rate accelerated. Absolute sea level rose avg 0.06 in/yr (1880–2013). Since 1993: 0.11–0.14 in/yr — roughly twice as fast as long-term trend.
Snow & ice (s22)Forms: sea, lake, river ice; snow cover; glaciers, ice caps, ice sheets; frozen ground. Altered in response to relatively minor temperature changes; reduced snow/ice influences air temperatures, sea level, ocean currents, storm patterns.
Arctic sea ice (s23–25)Dwindling; March = maximum, September = minimum monthly extent (1979–2016); melt season length has increased over the years (1979–2015); age of ice at minimum September week 1983–2015 (charts).
Glaciers (s26)Losing mass worldwide since at least the 1970s, contributing significantly to sea level rise; rate appears to have accelerated over roughly the last decade.

Impacts L5·s27–41

  • Health & society (s27–28): affects public health, agriculture, water supplies, energy, land use, recreation. Health impacts are complex, mostly indirect; health-related climate indicators are challenging and still emerging. Warmer temperatures → hotter days, longer heat waves → more heat-related illness and death; storms → flooding, high winds; reduced air quality, spreading diseases.
  • Chicago heat wave, Cook County, July 11–27, 1995 (s29): may have been hundreds more deaths than reported as "heat-related" on death certificates.
  • Lyme disease (bacterial) (s30): US incidence approx. doubled 1991→2014: 3.74 → 7.95 cases per 100,000.
  • West Nile virus (s31): neuroinvasive cases occur in <1% of infected people; affect brain / neurologic dysfunction (data 2002–2014).
  • Ecosystems (s32–35): provide food, clean water, services; climate affects water cycle, habitats, animal behaviour (nesting, migration), timing (flower blooms); risk of harm/extinction. Wildfires: disrupt ecosystems, damage property, risk to people, air pollution far from source. Streamflow: temperature, precipitation, snowpack, glaciers affect rate and timing of peak flow. Bird wintering ranges: North American birds shifted wintering grounds northward by avg >40 miles since 1966; several species by hundreds of miles (1966–2013).
  • Disasters 1998–2017 (s37–39, UNISDR): climate-related disasters are more significant; climate-related deaths are more in the last decade (charts).
  • COVID-19 (s40, s42): CO2 emission fall is only temporary; "Climate change is not on pause."

Key facts (WHO 2018) L5·s41

  • Climate change affects social & environmental determinants of health: clean air, safe drinking water, sufficient food, secure shelter.
  • 2030–2050: ~250,000 additional deaths/year from malnutrition, malaria, diarrhoea, heat stress.
  • Direct damage costs to health: USD 2–4 billion/year by 2030.
  • Reducing GHG via better transport, food, energy-use choices → improved health, particularly through reduced air pollution.
  • Conclusion (s42): climate change affects every country on every continent; sustainable development (essentially incl. Sustainable Transportation Systems) is the most feasible mitigation measure.

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Weather (hours) vs climate (hundreds to millions of years). Natural (not controllable) vs human (controllable) causes.
  • Oceans: positive (store CO2) vs negative (acidification, lower pH).
  • SST rise 0.13 °F/decade (1901–2015) vs sea level 0.06 in/yr (1880–2013) vs 0.11–0.14 in/yr (since 1993).
  • Lyme disease: bacterial; 3.74→7.95 per 100,000. West Nile neuroinvasive: <1%.
  • Warmest: 2016 (1st), 2020 (2nd), 2019 (3rd).

Lecture 6 — Impacts of Transportation Systems-I (30 slides)

Transport–environment link L6·s3–7

  • Transportation and environment = paradoxical: substantial socio-economic benefits but impacts environmental systems ("paradox of mobility and its costs").
  • Level of contribution: transport contributes (with other anthropogenic and natural causes) directly, indirectly, and cumulatively.
  • Scale of impact: local (noise and CO emissions) → global (climate change); also continental/national/regional (smog and acid rain).
  • Activities affecting the environment (s6): Infrastructure (resources, energy, land); Vehicle manufacture (resources & energy consumed); Vehicle operation (fossil fuel consumption); Vehicle maintenance (consumption & disposal of parts, lubricants); Vehicle disposal.

Energy L6·s8–13

  • Transport = about 20–25% of world energy use; strong correlation with level of development; 87% of world's total power production from fossil fuels.
  • World energy consumption (s10): Coal 39% (1965) → 28% (2016); Oil 40% (1965) → 33% (2016); Natural gas 15% (1965) → 24% (2016); Renewables 3% (2016).
  • Transport sector energy share (OECD, s11): 24.2% (1971) → 33.7% (2016).
  • Four major factors of energy use by transportation (s12): Vehicles (fuel efficiency); Infrastructure (transport efficiency); Demand (economic activity); Spatial infrastructure (average transport distance).
  • Fuel type (s13): oil-related fuels = 94%. Gasoline 43.5% most used; diesel 34.5%; heavy oil, biofuels, kerosene 21.8% [as written]; electricity and gases (e.g. LNG).

Emissions L6·s14–17

  • Energy generation = most significant contributor to global GHG; transport = 22% of global GHG emissions.
  • CO2 sharp increase after 1960s; China and India became the world's largest carbon emitters in early 21st century.
  • Global mean sea level (s17): main factors: increase in quantity of water in oceans; increase in global average temperatures. Increased risk of flooding & damage to coastal infrastructure.

Air quality & health L6·s19–22

  • Transport linked to climate change and air quality due to growing motorization and congestion.
  • Sources: highway vehicles, marine engines, locomotives, aircraft (gas + particulate emissions). Common pollutants: lead (Pb), carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen oxides (NOx), silicon tetrafluoride (SF6) [as written], benzene and volatile components (BTX), heavy metals (zinc, chrome, copper, cadmium), particulate matter (ash, dust).
  • Impact types (s22): Direct (noise pollution; CO emission); Indirect (secondary/tertiary) – higher consequence than direct (e.g. land clearing; particulates from incomplete combustion; respiratory & cardiovascular problems); Cumulative – additive, multiplicative or synergic (e.g. climate change).

Transport ↔ climate L6·s23–27

  • Fossil-fuel vehicles (cars, buses, 2 & 3 wheelers, steam trains, boats) → CO2 → greenhouse effect. Earth's average temperature up 0.8 °C since early 20th century [slide: "0.80C"].
  • s24: impacts of climate change on transport operations and infrastructure (figure). s25: carbon cycle – carbon pools in GtC; red = human emissions (figure).
  • Emissions by mode (s26): walking and cycling = best; sharing a vehicle reduces emissions vs single trips; mass/public transport has fewer emissions per trip.
  • Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) (s27): early stages of economic growth → pollution and degradation increase; beyond some level of income per capita the trend reverses → at high income, growth leads to environmental improvement (example fig: sulfur emissions).

Conclusion — GHG reduction measures L6·s28

  • Reduced use of private automobiles · increased use of public transport · alternate clean fuels · promotion of NMT · integrated transport development.

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • Impact types: direct vs indirect (higher consequence) vs cumulative (additive/multiplicative/synergic).
  • Scale: local (noise, CO) vs regional (smog, acid rain) vs global (climate change).
  • Transport = 20–25% of world energy use vs 22% of global GHG vs 33.7% of OECD energy (2016).

Lecture 7 — Impacts of Transportation Systems-II: Human Health (49 slides)

Transport and health L7·s3–10

  • Transport negatively impacts health in three broad ways: Traffic accidents; Environmental pollution; Spread of infectious diseases. (K. J. Button 2010)
  • 1989: International Agency for Research on Cancer concluded diesel and gasoline engine exhausts are probably carcinogenic to humans. 1998: California Air Resources Board identified diesel PM as a toxic air contaminant (TAC). Children near heavily travelled roads: ~50% higher risk of respiratory symptoms from ambient PM10.
  • Private motorcars → reduced physical activity; lack of physical activity = major risk for coronary heart disease (leading cause of mortality in Europe). Walking & cycling: promote health; decrease noise and air pollution.
  • s6 framework (Khreis 2017): Urban transport policy & planning (built environment; transport provision & mode choice) → motor vehicle crashes, air pollution, noise, heat islands, lack of green space and biodiversity loss, physical inactivity, social exclusion, community severance, climate change → premature mortality / morbidity.
  • Mental health (s8–10): improving walking, cycling, public transit improves mental health via increased physical activity and community cohesion. Increased walkability reduces symptoms of depression in older men and frequency of dementia in women and men. High-quality public transport reduces commute stress vs driving. Children able to play unhindered by traffic found socially active twice as much; lack hinders independence, linked to depression. Effects: risk-taking and aggressive behaviours; depression; post-traumatic effects of accidents; high traffic → social isolation (higher mortality and morbidity in elderly).

Accidents L7·s11–12

  • Globally every year >1.17 million die in road crashes; >10 million crippled or injured. ~70% involve young people. India ≈ 6% of global incidents. ~45,000 deaths in the US in 2006.
  • Among developed countries, per capita traffic fatalities increase with per capita vehicle travel; United States has highest per capita crash rate (OECD).

Traffic-generated air pollution L7·s13–16, 27–31

  • Motor vehicle traffic = main source of ground-level urban concentrations of hazardous air pollutants. CO and benzene inside cars ≈ 2–5 times higher than at roadside. Car users more exposed than pedestrians, cyclists, public transport users.
  • Main pollutants: PM (PM10, PM2.5); CO; NOx; NMVOCs; O3; benzene. Trace: CH4, CO2, SOx, TSPs.
  • Short-term PM10 effects: increased mortality; respiratory & cardiovascular diseases; more respiratory symptoms and medication use by asthmatics; reduced lung function. Recurrent cumulative exposure ↑ morbidity, ↓ life expectancy.
  • Determinants (s27): number of vehicles; age of vehicles; engine type & condition; type of fuel; meteorology; shape of urban environment; traffic organization.
  • Heavy vehicles = major polluters: diesel truck produces 50–100 times more fine and ultra-fine particles/km than a car with catalytic converter. Modern diesel emits less PM2.5 but more ultra-fine particles; ultra-fine particles (number rather than mass) suspected main cause of health effects → new diesels may cause more harm.
  • Health end-points (s29): mortality (long term); cardiovascular; respiratory; acute bronchitis (<15 y); asthma exacerbation (<15 y and ≥15 y); restricted physical activity (15–64 y); lung cancer.
  • Fine particulate pollution responsible for most of the 3–4 million annual air-pollution deaths worldwide (s31).

Los Angeles case L7·s17–26

  • People in LA county exposed to 60% more vehicular pollution than state average. Thick smog from the 1940s, lasted over 3 decades; LA = "City of Stars" also "City of Smog"; 1964/1966 photos (gas masks, low visibility, accidents). Clean Air Act passed 1970 → harsher control measures. LA used to be the most polluted air basin in US.

Health improvement strategies L7·s32–43

StrategyKey facts
WHO DPSEEA framework (s32)Driving force, Pressure, State, Exposure, Effect and Action.
Policy implications (s33–34)Unleaded petrol & catalytic converters helped. Holistic approach; tackling single pollutants in isolation can be counterproductive (e.g. promoting new diesel to cut CO2 → more ultra-fine particles). Co-benefits: consider the pollution mix; prioritise GHG actions that also reduce other pollutants like PM.
Traffic calming & speed control (s35)Reduces crash frequency & severity; reduces total vehicle travel. Per-km emissions decline from high to moderate speeds but increase at very low speeds (<20 km/h) and stop-and-go (e.g. speed bumps).
Active transport (s36)Better sidewalks, crosswalks, paths reduce crash risk; reduces total crash rates, air pollution, congestion; increases physical activity. 1% shift from motorized to NMT typically reduces emissions by 2–4%.
Public transit improvements (s37)More routes; longer operating hours; frequent services; quality vehicles & terminals; grade separations; real-time user information. Public transit travel has lower traffic fatality rate than other automobile travel.
Transport pricing reforms (s38)One-cent US state gasoline tax increase → per capita traffic fatalities −0.25%, per vehicle-mile −0.26% (Grabowski & Morrisey 2006). Sustained 20% gasoline price rise would prevent 2,000 crash deaths (5%) + 600 air pollution deaths (Leigh & Geraghty 2008). 1% vehicle travel reduction reduces crashes by more than 1% (Chi et al. 2010).
Mobility management marketing (s39)Programs encouraging shift from automobiles: commute trip reduction programs; transportation management associations; ride matching & vanpool support; direct marketing.
Smart growth (s40–43)Compact & mixed development; connected path/road networks; transport–land-use integration; walkability; efficient parking management. Residents drive 20–40% less; lower per capita casualty rates; more active transport. Caveat: compact development may increase some pollution exposure (canyon effect) → mitigate with cleaner transit vehicles & sidewalks away from traffic.

Case study: in-car aerosol exposure, 10 global cities L7·s44–46 (Kumar et al. 2021)

  • PM2.5 collected every 1 minute with portable laser particle counter from backseat. 3 car settings: windows-open; windows-closed with fan on; windows-closed with recirculation. Morning peak, evening peak, off-peak.
  • Hotspots: data points exceeding 90th percentile (P90) of 1-min PM2.5 during window-open setting. Compared with GDP, value of statistical life (VSL), road density.
  • Inference: Guangzhou (CAN) & Addis Ababa (ADD) hotspots = only ~26–28% of time but ~54–56% of inhaled dose. Hotspot length increases with road density; negative correlation with GDP and VSL (except Guangzhou).

Important Distinctions to Remember

  • 3 broad health impacts: accidents / pollution / infectious diseases.
  • IARC 1989 (probably carcinogenic) vs CARB 1998 (diesel PM = TAC).
  • Car interior CO & benzene 2–5× roadside; truck vs catalytic car 50–100× fine/ultra-fine particles.
  • 1% NMT shift → 2–4% emission cut; smart growth → drive 20–40% less; 20% gasoline price → 2,000 + 600 deaths.
  • Speed: per-km emissions fall high→moderate, rise <20 km/h.
